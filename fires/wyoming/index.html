What does a Wyoming fire map show right now?

A Wyoming fire map layers four independent feeds: agency incident records, mapped perimeters, satellite thermal detections and National Weather Service fire-weather alerts. Reading them together is the point, because each one is fresh in a different way.

Incident records hold the official figures: fire name, acres, percent contained, incident type and managing organization. They update when an agency files a report, which on a remote Wyoming fire can be many hours after the fire last moved.

Satellite detections fill that gap. NASA's MODIS and VIIRS instruments and NOAA's GOES satellites report heat within minutes to a few hours, which matters in a state where fires regularly start in country with no cell service. On the live map, each detection carries a probability tier based on whether an official record is linked and how recent the perimeter and heat signals are.

Confirm before you actA thermal detection is a heat measurement, not a confirmed wildfire. In Wyoming it can be a prescribed burn, agricultural burning or an energy facility flare. A hotspot with no official record attached is a question for your local interagency dispatch centre, not an answer.

When is Wyoming's wildfire season?

Wyoming's season runs from early summer into late autumn, and it can end weeks later than people expect. In 2024, the Pack Trail Fire ran from September 18 to November 2, and the Elk Fire from September 27 to November 14, both ended by snow rather than containment lines alone.

Peak activity clusters in August and September, when fine fuels are cured and wind events cross the plains. In 2025 the National Interagency Coordination Center recorded dry lightning affecting Wyoming on August 14 to 16, followed by the Red Canyon Fire in central Wyoming, which burned 124,709 acres between August 13 and September 18.

Which agencies fight wildfires in Wyoming?

The Wyoming State Forestry Division, part of the Office of State Lands and Investments, is the state wildland fire agency. Formed by the Legislature in 1952, it is responsible for fire management on 3.4 million surface acres of state trust land plus cooperative fire management on private and federal lands.

The division is headquartered in Cheyenne with field offices in Newcastle, Casper, Laramie, Riverton, Cody, Buffalo, Pinedale and Lyman, and employs 58 full-time and 28 seasonal staff plus five Good Neighbor Authority foresters. It publishes a Wyoming Wildfire Dashboard and an interactive fire map during the season.

Reporting a fire in Wyoming means calling one of four interagency dispatch centers or 911: Casper Interagency Dispatch, Cody Interagency Dispatch, Teton Interagency Dispatch or Great Plains Interagency Dispatch.

Wyoming sits in two geographic areasMost of Wyoming is coordinated through the Rocky Mountain Area Coordination Center in Lakewood, Colorado, but the Teton dispatch centre is a Great Basin Coordination Center member. That is why a fire in the northwest corner may appear in Great Basin situation reports while a fire near Gillette appears in Rocky Mountain ones.

Where do Wyoming wildfires usually burn?

Wyoming burns in two very different places. The mountain ranges, the Bighorns, the Bridger-Teton country and the Medicine Bow, carry conifer and produce long-duration fires. The northeast plains and the sagebrush-steppe carry grass, and produce fast wind-driven runs that cover ground in hours.

The 2024 season showed both. Four northeast Wyoming fires that started in the closing weeks of August, House Draw, Flat Rock, Remington and Constitution, collectively burned over 448,000 acres, much of it non-forested grassland and sagebrush-steppe, according to WyoFile.

Ownership complicates suppression. State Forester Kelly Norris told WyoFile that what distinguished 2024 was the amount of private and state land affected, which she expected to end up at "at least 75%" of the burned area.

What are Wyoming's largest wildfires on record?

The benchmark is 1988. The National Park Service reports that the Yellowstone fires of 1988 affected 36 percent of the park, 793,880 acres, with 42 of the fires caused by lightning and nine by humans, and that fires beginning outside the park burned roughly 500,000 acres of the total.

The Remington Fire crossed a state line. The National Interagency Coordination Center reported that it burned over 40 miles from Wyoming into Montana during a strong wind event on August 21 to 23, 2024.

Wyoming does not publish a verified all-time ranked list of state wildfires. Norris told WyoFile that there is no database showing with certainty how 2024 compares to seasons from decades past, and that recent numbers are more accurate because the state invested in software.

Why was 2024 Wyoming's worst season since 1988?

2024 combined a heavy grass crop with repeated wind events and late-season heat. Reporting agencies logged 620,069 acres in Wyoming, roughly ten times the 60,539 acres burned in neighbouring Colorado the same year.

"Having over 810,000 acres burned in Wyoming is massive," State Forester Kelly Norris told WyoFile on October 21, 2024. "Weather really drove these fires. We had storms coming in, we had wind, and we just had a lot of fuel. It was an extremely challenging fire season."

The Elk Fire rewrote a forest's records. Bighorn National Forest Supervisor Andrew Johnson told WyoFile that "in a matter of three hours it ran 25,000 acres," and that in the last 100 years the largest fire on the forest had been about 18,000 acres, taking over a month to reach that size.

Costs matched the acreage. Norris said the statewide tab could approach $200 million, with Wyoming and its local governments facing close to $60 million, and that the $39 million in the state's Emergency Fire Suppression Account had all been obligated.

What weather drives Wyoming fire danger?

Wyoming fire danger is a wind story first. Sustained wind across cured grass will move fire faster than fuel type or slope, which is why grassland fires here reach five figures of acreage in a single afternoon.

The National Weather Service offices in Cheyenne and Riverton issue Wyoming fire-weather products, and NWS Cheyenne maintains a Wyoming Fire Weather page that gathers current Red Flag Warnings, the significant wildland fire potential outlook, current air quality and the state's air quality monitoring network in one place.

Red Flag Warnings require critical fuels as well as critical weather. Published criteria at nearby NWS Grand Junction give a useful reference for the thresholds in play: relative humidity of 15 percent or less with sustained winds or frequent gusts of 25 mph or greater, occurring together for at least three hours in a 12-hour period, or widely scattered dry thunderstorms with 15 percent or more coverage.

How do evacuations work in Wyoming?

Wyoming evacuations are ordered by county sheriffs and county emergency management, and most counties use a three-level system. During the 2024 fires, Teton County Emergency Management issued evacuation orders and also reduced some areas to what WyoFile described as "a level-two warning, when residents are told to prepare for an evacuation but don't have to leave the area yet."

In practice the levels map to Ready, Set and Go, and Sheridan County moved neighbourhoods between them repeatedly as the Elk Fire shifted. Because the wording varies between counties, read the text of the alert rather than assuming a numbered level means the same thing everywhere.

Sign up before the seasonWyoming's counties push evacuation changes through their own alert systems, and rural cell coverage is patchy. Register your address with your county's system before fire season, keep a battery radio, and treat a level-two or warning-stage notice as your cue to load the vehicle rather than to wait for confirmation.

Fire restrictions are also county-level. In September 2026, the State Forestry Division reported that 19 counties were under some level of fire restriction, alongside Stage 1 restrictions on all BLM lands and on the Bridger-Teton and Medicine Bow-Routt National Forests.

How do you read a Wyoming fire map in 60 seconds?

Wyoming rewards a fixed reading order, because the fire that matters to you is often not the largest one on the screen.

  1. Open the live fire map and filter to Wyoming, or enable location to see fires within a set distance in miles.
  2. Find official perimeters first; a drawn perimeter means an agency has mapped and reported the fire.
  3. Open the nearest incident and read acres, containment, incident type and managing organization.
  4. Check the "Last NIFC update", "Last satellite heat" and "Last GOES heat" timestamps, which matter most in grass fuels.
  5. Look for Red Flag Warnings and Fire Weather Watches across your county and the county upwind.
  6. Confirm evacuation status for your own address with your county's alert system.

Smoke often arrives from outside the state, so compare with the Colorado, Utah and Nevada maps before assuming a local source, and see our guide to following current wildfires for the national picture.

Wyoming is the state where we most often see a fire outrun its own paperwork. A grass fire in the northeast can add tens of thousands of acres between two official updates, so on US Wildfire Tracker we put the age of every figure next to the figure itself: the last agency update, the last satellite heat detection and the last GOES signal. When those three timestamps disagree sharply, that gap is usually the most important thing on the screen.

What does wildfire smoke do to Wyoming air?

Wyoming's air quality alerts during fire season are usually driven by smoke, whether from local fires or from the Northwest and Northern Rockies. NWS Cheyenne links current air quality, the AirNow Fire and Smoke Map and the Wyoming Air Quality Monitoring Network from its fire weather page.

Smoke concentrations are reported through the EPA Air Quality Index, six color-coded categories in which a value of 100 corresponds roughly to the short-term national ambient air quality standard for a pollutant.

Frequently asked questions about the Wyoming fire map

How bad was Wyoming's 2024 fire season?

It was the state's largest in decades. Reporting agencies logged 738 wildfires and 620,069 acres, and State Forester Kelly Norris told WyoFile that over 810,000 acres had burned statewide, which she described as massive. She estimated the statewide cost could approach $200 million, with the state's $39 million Emergency Fire Suppression Account fully obligated.

What were the largest Wyoming fires in 2024?

The Remington Fire at 196,368 acres, which burned over 40 miles from Wyoming into Montana, and the House Draw Fire at 174,547 acres. The Elk Fire on the Bighorn National Forest reached 98,352 acres and the Pack Trail Fire on the Bridger-Teton reached 89,930 acres, both burning into November before autumn weather ended them.

How large were the 1988 Yellowstone fires?

The National Park Service reports that 36 percent of Yellowstone National Park, 793,880 acres, was affected in 1988. Forty-two of the fires were caused by lightning and nine by humans, and fires that began outside the park burned about 500,000 acres of the total. The park recorded its most severe drought that summer, with almost no measurable rain for nearly three months.

Who do I call to report a wildfire in Wyoming?

Call 911 or one of the four interagency dispatch centers listed by the Wyoming State Forestry Division: Casper Interagency Dispatch, Cody Interagency Dispatch, Teton Interagency Dispatch or Great Plains Interagency Dispatch. Those same centers are the authoritative source for incident information, and their names appear on official incident records shown on a fire map.

What do Wyoming evacuation levels mean?

Most Wyoming counties use a three-level system corresponding to Ready, Set and Go, issued by county sheriffs and emergency managers. WyoFile described a level-two warning during the 2024 Pack Trail Fire as the stage when residents are told to prepare for an evacuation but do not have to leave yet. Wording varies by county, so read the alert text rather than relying on the number.

Does lightning or people cause more Wyoming wildfires?

People start more fires; lightning burns far more acres. In 2024, reporting agencies recorded 420 human-caused and 318 lightning fires, but lightning accounted for 526,309 of the 620,069 acres. The 2025 split was similar, with lightning responsible for 164,292 of 172,810 acres. Remote lightning starts simply have more time to grow before crews arrive.

Why does Wyoming appear in two coordination centers?

Because the state is split between geographic areas. Most of Wyoming is coordinated by the Rocky Mountain Area Coordination Center in Lakewood, Colorado, while Teton Interagency Dispatch in the northwest is a member of the Great Basin Coordination Center. A single Wyoming fire may therefore be counted in whichever geographic area's situation report covers its dispatch centre.

How current are the numbers on a Wyoming fire map?

Satellite heat detections from VIIRS, MODIS and GOES arrive within minutes to a few hours of a pass. Official NIFC records and perimeters update on agency schedules, often once or twice a day on a large incident. US Wildfire Tracker refreshes automatically every few minutes while open and labels every figure with the time its source was last updated.
