What does an Illinois fire map show right now?

An Illinois fire map shows official incident records, satellite thermal detections and active National Weather Service fire-weather alerts together. In Illinois, a much larger share of that heat comes from agricultural burning and prescribed fire than from wildfire in the way Western states use the word.

The Illinois Emergency Management Agency and Office of Homeland Security defines a wildfire as "an unplanned, unwanted fire burning in a natural area, such as a forest, grassland, or prairie," and notes that in northern Illinois and Chicago these are usually called "Prairie Fires," while most of downstate Illinois calls them field or brush fires.

Satellite instruments — NASA's MODIS and VIIRS sensors and NOAA's GOES satellites — report heat within minutes to a few hours. On the live map, each detection carries a fire probability tier built from whether an official record is linked, how fresh any perimeter is, and how recent the satellite heat is.

Read Illinois hotspots carefullyA thermal detection is a measurement of heat, not a confirmed wildfire. In Illinois the same signature very often comes from an agricultural field burn, a planned prairie or savanna prescribed burn, or an industrial source. Treat an unconfirmed hotspot as a question, not an answer.

When is Illinois's wildfire season?

Illinois does not publish a single official wildfire season the way Western forestry agencies do. The clearest pattern comes from cured vegetation: dormant grass, dead leaf litter and crop residue burn fastest in spring, before green-up, and again in fall as fields cure after harvest.

The Nature Conservancy's Illinois program describes spring as the main burn window for prairie and savanna restoration, with a shorter fall window after plants go dormant. Grass fire risk on any dry, windy day is possible outside summer, when green vegetation elsewhere would normally suppress it.

Federal reporting shows how small Illinois's tracked wildfire load is. the National Interagency Coordination Center's annual report counts 40 fires burning 1,283 acres on federal- and state-protected land in Illinois in 2025, and 47 fires burning 441 acres in 2024 — all of it human-caused in both years, with zero fires attributed to lightning.

Those NICC figures only cover land protected by the US Forest Service, US Fish and Wildlife Service, and Illinois DNR. Most Illinois grass and field fires burn on land protected by local fire departments, which is not centrally tallied in a single public statewide count.

Which agencies respond to Illinois wildfires?

Illinois has no large state wildland firefighting agency comparable to a Western forestry-fire division. Local, township and rural fire protection districts are the primary responders to grass, field and brush fires statewide, the same departments that answer structure calls.

The Illinois Department of Natural Resources runs a Wildland Firefighter Program that trains and hires temporary crew members to NWCG standards — courses including L180, S130 and S190 plus FEMA coursework, an annual work-capacity test of a 3-mile walk in under 45 minutes carrying a 45-pound vest — mainly to protect state parks and natural areas and to deploy nationally, typically mid-June through mid-September for assignments of 18 or more days.

The Office of the Illinois State Fire Marshal leads statewide fire investigation, training standards and arson investigation rather than wildland suppression directly, and takes reports of an outdoor burn that has gone out of control. Federal land is protected by its own agency: the US Forest Service reported 29 to 39 fires a year on Illinois land it protects — chiefly the Shawnee National Forest in southern Illinois — in 2024 and 2025, and the US Fish and Wildlife Service protects national wildlife refuges.

Illinois is one of the states listed under NIFC's Eastern Area Coordination Center, which mobilizes crews and equipment across a twenty-state region when a request comes through the national system.

Where do Illinois wildfires usually burn?

Illinois fire activity clusters where cured fuel is continuous and wind can push it. Around Chicago and northern Illinois, remnant and restored prairie carries the fastest-moving grass fires — what IEMA calls "Prairie Fires." Downstate, most activity is agricultural: crop stubble, fencerows, ditch banks and pasture.

Forested fire risk is concentrated in the south, around the Shawnee National Forest, the state's only national forest, where the US Forest Service reports and suppresses fires on its own land. Illinois's landscape is otherwise dominated by row-crop agriculture, which limits the continuous fuel that carries a large wildfire in Western states.

Grass and cured prairie fire behaves differently than forest fire: it moves fast with wind, has a short flame residence time, and is usually contained quickly once wind drops — which is one reason Illinois's tracked fire counts stay small even in a busy spring.

Illinois does not maintain a public ranking of its largest wildfires the way states such as Colorado do. Why does Illinois rely on prescribed fire for prairies?

Prescribed fire, not wildfire suppression, is the defining fire story in Illinois. The state's remaining tallgrass prairie, savanna and woodland depend on repeated, planned burns to stay healthy, and land managers burn thousands of acres on purpose every year to keep that habitat from disappearing under brush.

The Nature Conservancy's Illinois program burned 12,350 acres on partner sites in 2023 through its southern Illinois burn crew alone, and its staff describe oak savanna as needing fire roughly every two to three years to stay open. Illinois DNR's Natural Heritage program similarly states that "prescribed fire is the most important management practice in maintaining and restoring healthy landscapes" and that the state needs significantly more of it.

Why prairie needs fire"If we don't use fire, our prairies, wetlands and oak woodlands become choked with dense brush — exotic shrubs and small trees — that end up degrading native habitat for lack of sunlight," says Bill Kleiman, Illinois fire manager and Nachusa Grasslands project director for The Nature Conservancy.

Burning is regulated and staffed on purpose. Under the Illinois Prescribed Burning Act, a certified prescribed burn manager must be on site with a written prescription while a burn is conducted, and Illinois DNR runs the certification program that trains and licenses those managers.

The training bottleneck"It's a huge hurdle to get enough people trained in prescribed fire, which is a critical tool to keeping our native habitats healthy," says Rob Littiken, project director at Kankakee Sands Preserves-Illinois for The Nature Conservancy, whose team has expanded burn-manager training to grow how much prairie, woodland and savanna can be treated each season.

Scale matters here. Forest Service reporting on Midewin National Tallgrass Prairie in Will County — the largest tallgrass prairie restoration in the country — describes roughly a third of the site, about 7,000 acres, treated with prescribed fire in an average year, concentrated in spring burn windows planned around wind, humidity and smoke dispersion.

Fire ecology research also shapes when burns happen. Elizabeth Bach, an ecosystem restoration scientist at Nachusa Grasslands for The Nature Conservancy, says: "We need prescribed fire for healthy habitats, and we need to protect box turtles. This paper gives Illinois managers some specific dates and parameters to make choices about how to prioritize prescribed fire in units where there may be box turtles."

For a fire map user, the practical effect is that a hotspot over a nature preserve, state park or national forest tract in spring is often a scheduled burn, not an emergency. Checking whether an official incident record is linked, and the burn's timing against typical spring or fall windows, helps tell the two apart. What weather drives Illinois fire danger?

Illinois fire danger turns on the same three ingredients as anywhere else: low relative humidity, sustained wind, and fuel dry enough to carry flame. The National Weather Service issues Red Flag Warnings only when those line up over cured grass, stubble or dry woodland litter.

NWS Chicago, based in Romeoville and covering northern Illinois and northwest Indiana, issues a Red Flag Warning for sustained 20-foot winds of 20 mph or higher, afternoon relative humidity under 25%, and 10-hour fuel moisture at 8% or less, expected within 24 hours; a Fire Weather Watch uses the same numbers up to 72 hours out. NWS Central Illinois, in Lincoln, publishes an identical threshold in its Annual Operating Plan for its own forecast area.

What changes seasonally is how often cured grass and crop residue make those thresholds matter — mainly before spring green-up and again after fall harvest.

How do evacuations and burn bans work in Illinois?

Evacuation orders in Illinois come from local authorities — county emergency management agencies, sheriffs and municipal officials — not from a single statewide staged system. IEMA's own guidance is direct rather than tiered: "If ordered to evacuate, leave immediately. Take your emergency kit." We found no published Illinois equivalent to a Ready-Set-Go or staged-level system; confirm terminology with your county before an event, not during one.

IEMA, based in Springfield, coordinates statewide response and runs a 24-hour response line, but the decision to order an evacuation sits with local government. Sign up for your county's emergency alert system in advance, since a fire map shows where fire and smoke are, not whether your specific address is under an order.

Open burning itself is tightly regulated. The Illinois Pollution Control Board and Illinois EPA prohibit open burning statewide under 35 Ill. Adm. Code Part 237 unless a specific exemption applies, and local governments can set stricter rules on top of state law.

What's exempt without a permitAgricultural waste from crop and livestock operations may be burned on the premises where it was produced, outside "restricted areas" — incorporated municipalities plus a one-mile buffer where population is 1,000 or more. Landscape waste has the same exemption except in counties over 400,000 people. Prairie and ecological landscape burns require a permit. Illinois EPA

How do you track Illinois fires in under a minute?

Because so much heat on an Illinois map is agricultural or prescribed rather than an emergency, reading it in a fixed order matters even more here than in a Western state. The sequence below works for a routine check or an active incident.

  1. Open the live fire map and filter to Illinois, or allow location access to see fires within a set distance in miles.
  2. Check the fire probability tier on any hotspot before assuming it is a wildfire — in Illinois it is often an agricultural or prescribed burn.
  3. Look for a linked official incident record. A record tied to a local fire department or agency is stronger evidence than heat alone.
  4. Check the timestamps — "Last NIFC update," "Last satellite heat" and "Last GOES heat" — to see how current each number actually is.
  5. Scan for an active NWS Red Flag Warning or Fire Weather Watch over your county, since that changes how fast grass fire can spread.
  6. Check your county's alert system and local news for any evacuation order covering your specific address.

The same routine works for smoke arriving from outside Illinois. If hotspots or a haze layer trace back across the state line, check the Oklahoma fire map or the Texas fire map for the grassland fires that often produce it, and see our guide to finding fires near you for the location-based view.

What does wildfire smoke do to Illinois air quality?

Illinois's most common wildfire-adjacent air quality problem is imported smoke rather than a local blaze. IEMA's own wildfire guidance notes that "smoke from fires outside of Illinois can blow in and affect those with medical or respiratory challenges," alongside local smoke from prescribed burns and agricultural field burning.

Smoke concentrations are reported through the EPA Air Quality Index, the same six-category scale used nationwide. An AQI of 100 corresponds roughly to the level of the short-term national ambient air quality standard for a pollutant.

Frequently asked questions about the Illinois fire map

How often does an Illinois fire map update?

Satellite thermal detections from VIIRS, MODIS and GOES arrive within minutes to a few hours of a satellite pass. Official incident records update when the responding agency files them, which for a small, fast-moving Illinois grass fire may be the only record that ever exists. US Wildfire Tracker refreshes automatically every few minutes and shows the last-updated time for each source it displays.

Does Illinois have a wildfire season like California or Colorado?

Not an official one. Illinois's fire activity tracks cured vegetation rather than a fixed calendar: spring, before green-up, is the busiest stretch for grass and prairie fire, with a smaller fall pickup after harvest as crop residue and fields cure. Any dry, windy day outside those windows can still carry fire through cured grass.

What is the largest wildfire in Illinois history?

Illinois does not maintain a public ranking of its largest wildfires the way Western states do. The clearest public figures come from NIFC's National Interagency Coordination Center, which recorded 40 fires burning 1,283 acres on federal- and state-protected Illinois land in 2025, and 47 fires burning 441 acres in 2024 — both entirely human-caused.

Who fights grass and field fires in Illinois?

Local, township and rural fire protection districts are the primary responders to grass, field and brush fires statewide. Illinois DNR trains and deploys a Wildland Firefighter Program crew mainly for state parks and natural areas, the US Forest Service protects the Shawnee National Forest, and the Illinois State Fire Marshal leads statewide investigation and training standards rather than direct suppression.

Is that hotspot on the map a wildfire or a prescribed burn?

In Illinois, often a prescribed burn or agricultural field burn rather than a wildfire. Land managers such as The Nature Conservancy and Illinois DNR burn thousands of acres of prairie and savanna on purpose each spring and fall. Check the fire probability tier and whether an official incident record is linked before treating a hotspot as an emergency.

What are Illinois's open burning rules?

The Illinois EPA and Pollution Control Board prohibit open burning statewide under 35 Ill. Adm. Code Part 237 unless an exemption applies. Agricultural and landscape waste can generally be burned on-site outside "restricted areas" — municipalities plus a one-mile buffer where population is 1,000 or more — and prairie or ecological burns need a permit. Local governments can impose stricter limits.

Does Illinois use Ready-Set-Go or staged evacuation levels?

We found no published Illinois equivalent to Ready-Set-Go or a numbered evacuation-level system. IEMA's own guidance is plain language — if ordered to evacuate, leave immediately — and the order itself comes from your county emergency management agency or sheriff, not the state. Confirm your county's specific terminology and alert system before you need it.

Why does Illinois report zero lightning-caused fires?

NIFC's annual reports show every fire recorded on federal- and state-protected Illinois land in 2024 and 2025 was human-caused, with zero attributed to lightning in either year. That matches Illinois's fire profile generally: most starts trace to agricultural burning, debris burning or accidental ignition in cured grass rather than dry-lightning events common in Western states.
