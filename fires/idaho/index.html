What does an Idaho fire map show right now?

An Idaho fire map layers four public feeds over one basemap: official incident records from the National Interagency Fire Center, mapped fire perimeters, satellite thermal detections from NASA's VIIRS and MODIS instruments and NOAA's geostationary GOES analysis, and active National Weather Service fire-weather alerts.

Satellite heat is the fast layer. Polar-orbiting passes cross Idaho several times a day and the geostationary analysis fills the gaps, so a fresh thermal signal with no matching incident record usually means a start that has not reached a dispatch record yet — common in the Frank Church wilderness country where roads are scarce.

US Wildfire Tracker fuses those feeds on its free live fire map and attaches a fire probability tier to every detection, blending whether an official record is linked, how fresh the perimeter is and how recent the heat is. Weak signals below 25 percent stay hidden by default because they often reflect prescribed burns or industrial heat.

When is Idaho's closed fire season?

Idaho runs a legally defined closed fire season rather than an informal one. Valley County's fire warden office states the rule plainly: "The period from May 10th to October 20th of each year, has been designated as the closed season for fires in the state of Idaho."

Inside that window a burn permit is required. The same guidance notes that during the closed season "it is unlawful for any person to set, or cause to be set, a fire without first having obtained a Burn Permit," available from the local protective district fire warden or the state burn permit website. Campfires are the exception unless critical fire danger closes them too.

Large-fire activity peaks later than the permit season starts. Southern Idaho rangeland typically becomes receptive in late June once cheatgrass cures, while the mountain timber of central and northern Idaho reaches its driest point between late July and mid-September.

Which agencies fight wildfires in Idaho?

Idaho's wildfire response is unusually federal. The Bureau of Land Management describes its Idaho operation as "one of the largest and most complex fire programs in the BLM, protecting approximately 11 million acres of range and forest land in central and southern Idaho and employing nearly 500 people."

That program alone responds to an average of 330 fires burning about 270,000 acres a year, with roughly 50 fire engines, two helicopters, four single engine air tankers, 11 bulldozers, an interagency hotshot crew and four interagency dispatch centers.

The Idaho Department of Lands runs the state program. IDL describes its fire arm as working "in partnership with two Timber Protection Associations and with the support of rural volunteer fire departments and other partners" and as "responsible for fire suppression and prevention on more than 9 million acres of state, private and federal land in Idaho," organized into 12 forest protective districts.

The state's stated tactical goal is aggressive initial attack. IDL says its fire program "focuses on initial attack with the goal of keeping fires at 10 acres or less," a standard that matters because the endowment timber it protects funds Idaho schools.

Rangeland Fire Protection Associations fill the gap on open country. IDL describes them as "nonprofit volunteer firefighting organizations made up of local farmers, ranchers, and landowners," and reports that Idaho's ten RFPAs "protect a combined 9.3 million acres of private, state and federal rangeland." Idaho's first was formed in 2012.

Who to tell first if you see smokeBLM Idaho asks the public to call 911 immediately for any fire they start or spot, and lists Boise Dispatch and South Central Idaho Dispatch among its reporting contacts. Prevention guidance for public lands covers spark arrestors, target shooting and vehicles driven into tall grass — see BLM Idaho Fire Information.

Why is Idaho split between two fire regions?

Idaho is the only state in the Northwest whose wildfire coordination is split between two of the country's ten geographic areas. Northern Idaho belongs to the Northern Rockies geographic area, and southern Idaho belongs to the Great Basin.

The Northern Rockies Coordination Center in Missoula describes its boundary as covering "the States of Montana, North Dakota, and northern Idaho, Yellowstone National Park, and a small portion of South Dakota." The Idaho Department of Lands is listed among its primary cooperating agencies.

The Great Basin Coordination Center in Salt Lake City draws the other half of the line, stating that its geographic area "encompasses Utah, Nevada, Idaho-south of the Salmon River, the western Wyoming mountains and the Arizona Strip." Its Idaho zone centers are Boise, Central Idaho, Eastern Idaho, Payette and South Idaho.

This matters when you read a map. Two adjacent fires can be managed under different coordinating groups with different resource queues, which is one reason containment percentages on either side of the Salmon River can move at very different speeds. Neighbouring Montana sits entirely inside the Northern Rockies area.

Idaho hosts the national hubThe National Interagency Fire Center, the country's interagency wildland fire coordination hub, is in Boise at 3833 S. Development Avenue. The Northern Rockies Coordination Center describes NIFC as the focal point for overseeing interagency coordination across all ten geographic areas — see NRCC, About the Northern Rockies Area.

Where do Idaho wildfires usually burn?

Idaho burns in two very different landscapes. Southern Idaho's Snake River Plain and Owyhee country is sagebrush and grass, where wind-driven fires cover enormous ground quickly. Central and northern Idaho is mountain timber, where fires burn longer and are harder to reach.

Access shapes outcomes as much as fuel does. Central Idaho contains some of the largest roadless country in the lower 48, so a lightning start there may burn for days before crews can reach it, while a rangeland start near Interstate 84 draws engines within minutes.

BLM's fuels program reflects that reality, treating more than 100,000 acres a year with mechanical thinning, prescribed fire, seeding and fuel breaks intended to give suppression crews a place to stop a running rangeland fire.

What are the largest Idaho wildfires ever?

Idaho's defining fire is the Great Fire of 1910, the event that burned across the Panhandle and into western Montana in a single August. Modern Idaho megafires are smaller but still enormous by national standards, and most of them burn in the southern rangeland or the central mountains.

What researchers say about Idaho's high-country firesBoise State geoscience faculty member Jen Pierce, whose research reconstructs 8,000 years of fire history in the Sawtooths, told her university that fire "is a natural part of our ecosystem" and that burned places "will be changed and they'll be beautiful in their own new way." The same December 4, 2024 article notes the Wapiti Fire "burned through forests which used to be too wet to burn."

What weather drives Idaho fire danger?

Idaho's worst fire days combine strong wind, very low relative humidity and fuels already cured by summer heat. When the National Weather Service expects those to coincide, it issues a Red Flag Warning for the affected fire-weather zones.

The NWS defines a Red Flag Warning as an alert issued "in conjunction with land management agencies, to alert land managers to an ongoing or imminent critical fire weather pattern," used when conditions are occurring or expected shortly. A Fire Weather Watch is the earlier stage, meaning critical conditions are "possible but not imminent or occurring."

Three forecast offices split the state: Boise for southwest Idaho, Pocatello for the southeast and Spokane for the Panhandle. Their fire-weather zones follow terrain rather than county lines, so a warning can cover a valley floor while the ridge above it stays out.

Dry lightning is the other driver. A single storm crossing the Salmon River country can leave dozens of holdover starts smouldering in timber, which is why the number of incidents on a map can climb for days after the storm has passed.

How do evacuations work in Idaho?

Idaho standardized its wildfire evacuation language in 2026. Teton County Emergency Management states that "The State of Idaho is adopting the 'Ready, Set, Go' wildfire evacuation program as the statewide standard for public evacuation messaging, establishing a unified, consistent approach to communicating evacuation risk and action levels across all jurisdictions."

Before that, counties used different systems. Reporting on the change quoted officials describing a patchwork in which some counties used "3-2-1" and others used red, yellow and green, which meant the same warning could mean different things one county line apart.

Authority did not change with the vocabulary. Teton County notes that the messaging "aligns with the statutory authority of Idaho's county sheriffs, who retain responsibility for issuing evacuation orders within their respective counties." Older Level 1, 2 and 3 phrasing still appears in some North Idaho reporting and maps onto Ready, Set and Go respectively.

What the state says Go meansMichael McManus, deputy division administrator of fire for the Idaho Department of Lands, described the point of one statewide standard this way: "Whether you're up near Canada or down near Nevada, you're going to understand that if it's go, that means that it's a life safety threat and it's time to get out." He added that in Idaho "you should probably be in ready all the time" — KMVT, May 10, 2026.

How do you read an Idaho fire map fast?

Checking Idaho fire activity should take under a minute. This sequence works on the live map or in the Chrome side panel, and it puts proximity ahead of headline acreage.

  1. Open the map and filter to Idaho, or allow location access to see only fires within a set distance in miles of where you are.
  2. Read the fire probability tier before the acreage. Weak signals below 25 percent are hidden by default because they often reflect prescribed burns or industrial heat.
  3. Open the nearest incident and compare three timestamps: last NIFC update, last satellite heat and last GOES heat. Fresh heat under an old perimeter means the fire is still moving.
  4. Check whether a Red Flag Warning polygon covers your area, since that changes how quickly conditions can deteriorate.
  5. Confirm evacuation status with your county sheriff or emergency management office, then use the directions-away links if you need to move.

What does wildfire smoke do to Idaho air?

Smoke reaches far more Idahoans than fire does. The state's north-south valleys act as smoke traps: the Treasure Valley, the Palouse and the Panhandle lakes country all collect smoke under late-summer inversions, sometimes from fires hundreds of miles away in Washington, Oregon or British Columbia.

Air quality is reported on the Air Quality Index. The EPA describes it as six color-coded categories, noting that for each pollutant "an AQI value of 100 generally corresponds to an ambient air concentration that equals the level of the short-term national ambient air quality standard for protection of public health."

The state runs a formal response for those episodes. Idaho DEQ says that when wildfire smoke threatens the state it implements the Wildfire Smoke Event Response Protocol, coordinating with health districts, five tribes, federal agencies and the neighbouring Montana, Oregon and Washington air agencies, and that it "provides a smoke forecast when wildfire smoke impacts Idaho."

DEQ's monitoring network backs that up with instruments "at more than 30 sites statewide," and its data flows hourly to EPA's AirNow. A clear local fire map can still coincide with hazardous air, because the plume that matters may have travelled from another state. Frequently asked questions about the Idaho fire map

How often does an Idaho fire map update?

It depends on the layer. Satellite thermal detections arrive several times a day from polar-orbiting instruments, with NOAA geostationary heat filling gaps between passes. Official incident records and perimeters follow agency schedules, often once per operational period. US Wildfire Tracker refreshes automatically every few minutes while open and shows the last-update time for each source.

Why is Idaho covered by two coordination centers?

Because the national geographic area boundaries cut through the state. Northern Idaho falls inside the Northern Rockies geographic area coordinated from Missoula, while southern Idaho falls inside the Great Basin, whose Idaho dispatch zones are Boise, Central Idaho, Eastern Idaho, Payette and South Idaho. Statewide totals therefore have to be assembled from two separate sets of statistics.

Is a Red Flag Warning the same as a fire?

No. A Red Flag Warning is a National Weather Service alert about weather, issued with land management agencies when a critical fire weather pattern is occurring or expected shortly. It means a new ignition would spread dangerously, not that a fire is burning. A Fire Weather Watch is the earlier stage, meaning those conditions are possible but not yet imminent.

Where is the National Interagency Fire Center?

In Boise, Idaho, at 3833 S. Development Avenue. NIFC is the national hub for interagency wildland fire coordination and is the focal point for overseeing coordination across the ten geographic areas. Its incident records and interagency perimeter services are one of the core data layers behind any credible US fire map, including this one.

Does the map show fires in wilderness areas?

Yes, and that is where satellite layers earn their keep. Large parts of central Idaho are roadless, so a lightning start can burn for days before anyone reports it. Thermal detections from VIIRS, MODIS and GOES will show the heat regardless of whether an incident record exists yet, though small or smouldering fires under cloud can still be missed.

Do I need a burn permit in Idaho?

Yes, during the closed fire season. Idaho law designates May 10 through October 20 as the closed season, and during it burning outside city limits without a permit from the local protective district fire warden is unlawful. Campfires are exempt unless prohibited during critical fire danger. The director of lands can extend the closed season when conditions stay dangerous, and has done so.

Can I track Idaho and Montana fires together?

Yes. The national view shows every state at once, which is useful along the Bitterroot Divide where the same coordinating group manages fires on both sides of the state line. You can also filter to a single state, or use the fires-near-me view, which works on distance in miles and ignores state boundaries entirely.
