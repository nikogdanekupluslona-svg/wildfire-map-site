What does a Kansas fire map show right now?

A Kansas fire map layers official incident records, mapped perimeters, satellite thermal detections and National Weather Service fire-weather alerts. Reading all four together matters more in Kansas than almost anywhere else, because a grass fire can add thousands of acres between two official updates.

Incident records carry the confirmed numbers: fire name, acres, percent contained, incident type and managing organization. On a rural Kansas grass fire, those figures often lag the fire itself, since reports are filed by responding fire departments between calls, not continuously.

Satellite detections close that gap. NASA's MODIS and VIIRS instruments and NOAA's GOES satellites report heat within minutes to a few hours. On the live map, each detection carries a probability tier based on whether an official record is linked and how recent the perimeter and heat signals are.

Confirm before you actA thermal detection is a heat measurement, not a confirmed wildfire. In Kansas it can be a Flint Hills prescribed burn, an agricultural stubble burn or a flare. A hotspot with no official record attached is a question for your county emergency manager, not an answer.

When is Kansas's wildfire season?

Kansas's most active wildfire window runs from late February through April, before spring green-up. Dormant, cured grass from the prior growing season combines with strong winds and low humidity to produce fast-moving fires, and a late freeze after early green-up can leave newly green grass killed back and flammable again.

A second, quieter risk period follows summer curing in late fall, though spring remains the state's defining fire season. Kansas experiences at least several thousand wildfires in a typical year, and state officials have called it one of the most fire-active states in the country by incident count, largely because grass ignites and spreads so readily on the plains.

A record season, liveOn May 14, 2026 at 5:08 p.m., Governor Laura Kelly issued a verbal state of disaster emergency for wildland fires in Clark, Ford, Harper, Meade and Morton counties after dry lightning touched off at least 17 grass fires overnight. "Weather conditions are such that a high risk exists for wildland fires," Kelly said, urging residents to "use extreme caution and avoid burning." Source: Kansas Adjutant General's Department.

Which agencies fight fires in Kansas?

The Kansas Forest Service, an agency within K-State Research and Extension at Kansas State University, has supported local fire suppression since 1963. It trains firefighters, distributes surplus federal equipment to rural departments, runs a Wildfire Risk Assessment Portal and assists with prescribed burning, but initial attack is almost always local.

Initial attack in Kansas is carried out by county and township fire departments, most of them volunteer. The Kansas Division of Emergency Management, part of the Adjutant General's Department, coordinates state-level support once a fire outgrows local capacity, staffing the State Emergency Operations Center with the Kansas Forest Service, the Office of the State Fire Marshal, the Kansas Highway Patrol and the National Weather Service.

During the May 2026 southwest Kansas outbreak, that coordination put four Kansas Army National Guard Black Hawk helicopters, more than 90 fire trucks from 75 departments, over 200 firefighters and out-of-state engine strike teams into Clark and Meade counties within two days. "The Kansas Forest Service and our state partners are actively supporting response efforts to several large wildfires in southwest Kansas," said Bill Waln, Fire Management Officer for the Kansas Forest Service.

No department fights this alone"No fire department has the resources to handle a major conflagration like this," State Fire Marshal Mark Engholm said of the May 2026 fires. Source: Kansas Adjutant General's Department.

Kansas sits within the Rocky Mountain Area Coordination Center's geographic area alongside Colorado, Wyoming, Nebraska and South Dakota. When Kansas requests out-of-state crews, engines or aircraft, that request is filled through the Rocky Mountain center in Lakewood, Colorado, the same coordination hub used by its Great Plains and Rocky Mountain neighbors.

Where do Kansas wildfires usually burn?

Kansas wildfires concentrate in two bands: the Red Hills and Gyp Hills of south-central Kansas, and the Flint Hills tallgrass prairie running from the Nebraska line to Oklahoma through the eastern third of the state. Both are cattle country dominated by native grass rather than crops, which means continuous, curable fuel across huge unbroken distances.

Southwest and south-central Kansas, Clark, Comanche, Meade, Barber and Harper counties, have produced the state's largest fires, because rangeland there is extensive, winds are strong and humidity drops fast behind a cold front. The same geography that makes it prime grazing land makes it prime fire country when conditions align.

Cropland in central and western Kansas burns too, especially wheat stubble and Conservation Reserve Program grass, but those fires are typically smaller and more containable than open rangeland runs, where a single wind-driven fire can cross county lines in an afternoon.

Why do ranchers burn the Flint Hills every spring?

Every spring, ranchers across the Flint Hills deliberately burn tallgrass prairie to control invasive woody plants and boost cattle forage, producing one of the largest concentrated prescribed-fire seasons in North America. The Kansas Forest Service describes the purpose plainly: prescribed burning "reduces fuel loading, controls unwanted trees and brush, increases forage value, and rejuvenates our grasslands."

Burning concentrates in March and April, filling the sky over eastern Kansas with smoke for a period each spring. Because so much burning happens in such a short window, the Kansas Department of Health and Environment administers a Flint Hills Smoke Management Plan with ranchers, conservation groups and Kansas State University to steer smoke away from downwind cities when possible.

Not every hotspot is an emergencyKDHE runs a smoke-modeling tool each burn season to forecast where prescribed-fire smoke will travel. "We are entering the 16th year that we have been able to provide this important tool for the prescribed fire community," said Douglas Watson, a meteorologist with KDHE's Bureau of Air. Source: KDHE.

This is also why a Kansas fire map fills with heat signatures every March and April that are not wildfires at all. A satellite detection during Flint Hills season is far more likely to be a planned pasture burn than an uncontrolled fire, which is exactly why linking each detection to an official record matters most during burn season.

What are the largest wildfires in Kansas history?

The largest fire in Kansas's recorded history is the March 2017 Starbuck Fire, part of a four-fire Northwest Oklahoma Complex that ignited March 6, 2017, and burned a combined 779,292 acres across Oklahoma and Kansas. The Kansas Forest Service put the Kansas-side total at 463,887 acres, mostly in Clark County with additional burning in Comanche and Meade counties.

The Starbuck Fire destroyed 26 homes and killed large numbers of cattle as it raced toward Englewood, Ashland and Sitka. "You could even burn water," Englewood fire chief and rancher Bernie Smith told the High Plains Journal, describing how completely cured the grass was that week.

The Anderson Creek Fire the year before had itself been the largest fire in Kansas history at the time, starting in Woods County, Oklahoma, on March 22, 2016, before crossing into Comanche and Barber counties, fueled by roughly 45 mph wind and heavy eastern red cedar encroachment. No official statewide ranked list beyond these two events was found; Kansas agencies describe Starbuck as the largest fire on record.

What weather drives Kansas fire danger?

Kansas fire danger is a wind-and-curing story more than a drought story. The National Weather Service's Grassland Fire Danger Index, used across the Central Plains, weighs wind speed, wind gusts, relative humidity and temperature, but treats how "cured," or dried out, the grass is as the dominant factor: fully cured, brown grass spreads fire far faster than green grass regardless of wind.

NWS Wichita publishes the Grassland Fire Danger Index on a scale where low values mean a fire is stopped easily by a road or a water truck, and values above 50 mean direct attack generally fails outright. Red Flag Warnings are typically reserved for the worst 5 percent of fire-danger days in a season, though NWS offices can issue one at a lower value if fire-weather partners request it.

The office explicitly cautions that its Grassland Fire Danger Index does not account for rainfall or snow already on the ground, so forecasters and dispatchers adjust it against current soil and fuel moisture rather than reading the number alone.

How do evacuations work in Kansas?

Kansas county emergency managers issue two-tier alerts: an evacuation order, meaning leave immediately because danger is imminent, and an evacuation warning, meaning be ready to leave at a moment's notice, useful extra time for anyone moving livestock or with mobility needs. Wording is set locally rather than through one statewide numbered system, so read the alert text rather than assuming a term means the same thing in every county.

During the May 2026 outbreak, Clark County Emergency Management ordered Englewood evacuated as fire approached from Beaver, Oklahoma, and Meade State Park was closed with all weekend reservations cancelled. Rural Kansas evacuations frequently involve moving cattle herds as well as people, which is why ranchers are often still on their land arranging livestock right up until an order is issued.

Register before fire seasonSign up for your county's emergency alert system before spring, since rural cell coverage is inconsistent and a wind-driven grass fire can close the gap between warning and order in under an hour.

How do you track a Kansas fire in under a minute?

Kansas rewards speed of reading more than most states, because a grass fire's footprint can double before an official update posts.

  1. Open the live fire map and filter to Kansas, or enable location to see fires within a set distance in miles.
  2. Check whether the detection has an official record attached; an unlinked hotspot during March or April is often a Flint Hills prescribed burn.
  3. Open the nearest incident and read acres, containment, incident type and managing organization.
  4. Compare the "Last NIFC update," "Last satellite heat" and "Last GOES heat" timestamps; a large gap between them matters more in grass fuels than in timber.
  5. Check for Red Flag Warnings or a Grassland Fire Danger outlook across your county and the county upwind.
  6. Confirm evacuation status for your own address directly with your county's alert system, not from the map alone.

Fires can also drift in from Oklahoma along the same wind events that drive Kansas outbreaks, so compare with the Oklahoma and Colorado maps, and see our guide to wildfire tracking for how to build a daily routine around it.

Kansas is the state where the gap between an official update and reality grows fastest. A grass fire in the Red Hills can add tens of thousands of acres in an afternoon that a fire department hasn't had time to report yet, so on US Wildfire Tracker we show the age of every figure next to the figure itself. When satellite heat is minutes old but the last agency update is hours old, treat the satellite signal as the one worth acting on.

What does wildfire smoke do to Kansas air quality?

Kansas smoke has two very different sources: uncontrolled wildfire and the Flint Hills' own planned prescribed burning, and both are reported through the same EPA Air Quality Index used nationwide. A value of 100 corresponds roughly to the short-term national air quality standard for a given pollutant, and Kansas cities downwind of the Flint Hills, including Wichita and Kansas City, can see measurable particulate spikes for days each spring even when no wildfire is burning.

Frequently Asked Questions About the Kansas Fire Map

What is the largest wildfire in Kansas history?

The March 2017 Starbuck Fire, part of the four-fire Northwest Oklahoma Complex that burned 779,292 acres across Oklahoma and Kansas combined. The Kansas Forest Service attributes 463,887 acres of that total to Kansas, mostly in Clark County, making it the largest fire recorded in state history.

When does Kansas's wildfire season peak?

Late February through April, before spring green-up, when cured grass, strong wind and low humidity combine. A late freeze after early green-up can extend the risk further into spring by killing new growth back to flammable dry grass.

Why does the Flint Hills burn every spring on purpose?

Ranchers deliberately burn tallgrass prairie in March and April to control invasive woody plants such as eastern red cedar and to improve cattle forage. The Kansas Department of Health and Environment runs a Flint Hills Smoke Management Plan and a seasonal smoke-modeling tool to reduce the impact on downwind cities.

How do I know if a Kansas hotspot on a fire map is a wildfire or a prescribed burn?

Check whether an official incident record is linked to the satellite detection. During March and April especially, an unlinked hotspot in the Flint Hills is more likely to be a planned pasture burn than an uncontrolled wildfire; a linked record with growing acreage and an assigned managing agency is the stronger signal of an actual incident.

Who fights wildfires in Kansas?

Local county and township fire departments, mostly volunteer, handle initial attack. The Kansas Forest Service supports them with training and equipment, and the Kansas Division of Emergency Management coordinates state resources, including National Guard helicopters and out-of-state strike teams, once a fire outgrows local capacity.

What do Kansas evacuation orders and warnings mean?

An evacuation order means leave immediately because danger is imminent. An evacuation warning means be ready to leave at a moment's notice, giving extra time to move livestock or handle mobility needs. Wording is set at the county level rather than through one statewide system, so always read the alert text.

How current is the data on a Kansas fire map?

Satellite heat detections from VIIRS, MODIS and GOES arrive within minutes to a few hours of a pass. Official incident records update whenever a responding department files a report, which can lag well behind a fast-moving grass fire. US Wildfire Tracker refreshes automatically every few minutes and labels each figure with the time its source last updated.

Does lightning or human activity start more Kansas wildfires?

Human activity, including debris burning, equipment sparks and vehicle-caused ignitions, starts most Kansas fires, though dry lightning triggers some of the largest outbreaks, including the 17 fires that prompted a May 2026 disaster declaration across five southwest Kansas counties.
