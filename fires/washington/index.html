What does a Washington fire map show right now?

A Washington fire map layers four separate public feeds over one basemap: official incident records from the National Interagency Fire Center, mapped fire perimeters, satellite thermal detections from NASA and NOAA, and active National Weather Service fire-weather alerts. Each layer answers a different question, and each one ages at a different speed.

Satellite detections move faster. Polar-orbiting instruments on the VIIRS and MODIS platforms pass over Washington several times a day, and NOAA's geostationary GOES analysis adds heat detections between those passes. A hotspot that appears without a matching incident record usually means a new start that agencies have not yet catalogued.

US Wildfire Tracker combines all of that on its free live fire map, then attaches a fire probability score to each detection. The score fuses whether an official record is linked, how fresh the perimeter is and how recent the satellite heat is, so a stale record and a 20-minute-old thermal signal do not look identical.

A map is not an evacuation orderWashington's Emergency Management Division is explicit that "Most evacuations are decided at the local level." Sheriffs and fire districts issue the actual orders. Use any map to understand the situation, then act on what your county tells you — see the Washington Military Department wildfire page.

When is Washington's wildfire season?

Washington's wildfire season is usually described in two ways. The state Emergency Management Division says the season "usually begins in early July and typically culminates in early October when regular rain returns to the Northwest," while DNR staffs its own crews on a longer window, "generally operational April through October."

The peak is narrower than either window. Washington's state hazard mitigation plan states that "Lightning generally is the cause of most fires in the peak fire period of July, August and early September," and that the early and late shoulders of the season are usually human-caused fires.

The season also runs longer east of the crest. The same plan explains that "The fire season typically is longer in Eastern Washington than in Western Washington because the eastern side is drier and has a larger number of ignition sources, primarily the number of lightning strikes."

West of the crest, the Douglas fir and hemlock forests are usually too wet to burn at scale. The exception is a dry east-wind event in late summer or early fall, which is what turned Labor Day 2020 into one of the worst fire days in modern state history.

The human-caused share is the number that matters most for prevention. In its 2025 Wildfire At-A-Glance summary, DNR reports that 90 percent of all wildfires in Washington are human-caused, leaving lightning responsible for the remaining tenth.

During the season DNR also runs Industrial Fire Precaution Levels, described by the agency as "an activity closure system to reduce wildfire risk" that applies by law to woods workers and industrial forest users on 13 million acres of unimproved private and state forestland.

Which agencies fight wildfires in Washington?

Wildfire response in Washington is split by land ownership. The Department of Natural Resources protects more than 13 million acres of private and state-owned forest land, the US Forest Service covers the national forests, the Bureau of Land Management and Bureau of Indian Affairs cover their own holdings, and 567 local fire districts handle everything else.

DNR describes itself as "Washington state's wildfire fighting force, with more than 1,300 employees trained and available to fight fires as needed," including more than 800 permanent staff, 550 seasonal employees, about 120 wildland fire engines, nine helicopters and six single engine air tankers under contract.

DNR is led by the Commissioner of Public Lands, a statewide elected official. Dave Upthegrove is Washington's fifteenth Commissioner of Public Lands since statehood in 1889.

When a fire outgrows local capacity, a separate system takes over. Under state law the Fire Service Resource Mobilization Plan supplies personnel and equipment beyond local means; only a local fire chief can request it, the Chief of the Washington State Patrol approves it, and the State Fire Marshal serves as the state fire resources coordinator.

What Washington's Commissioner of Public Lands says"Strong working partnerships across the local, state and federal levels are so critical when it comes to fighting wildfire. I'm committed to ensuring Washington has the resources needed to protect our lands and communities." — Dave Upthegrove, Commissioner of Public Lands, in the DNR Wildfire At-A-Glance.

Above the individual agencies sits the Northwest Interagency Coordination Center in Portland, which serves Oregon and Washington together. Its published mission is to act as "the northwest area geographic focal point to provide logistical support and intelligence relative to anticipated and ongoing wildfire activity" for federal and cooperating state agencies.

Evacuations are not a firefighting agency's job. DNR states plainly that "Local emergency management offices, such as sheriff departments or local fire districts, order and communicate evacuations when needed." The same division of labor applies in Oregon and across the Northern Rockies.

Where do Washington wildfires usually burn?

Most of Washington's large wildfire acreage burns east of the Cascade crest. The Okanogan and Methow valleys, the Columbia Basin shrub-steppe, the east slopes of the Cascades and the pine country around Spokane and the Colville National Forest carry the great majority of the state's big-fire history.

The imbalance is extreme. University of Washington researchers note that "more than 99 percent of wildfires in the last 40 years have been east of the Cascade Crest," and the Emergency Management Division puts about 70 percent of the roughly 900 fires the state fights each year in Eastern Washington.

What are the largest Washington wildfires ever?

Two different fires hold two different records. The Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife calls the 2020 Cold Springs Canyon and Pearl Hill fire "the largest wildfire in Washington State recorded history," while the 2014 Carlton Complex remains the largest single fire to burn entirely within the state.

What weather drives Washington fire danger?

Three ingredients drive Washington's worst fire days: strong wind, very low relative humidity, and fuels that have already dried out. When the National Weather Service expects those to line up, it issues a Red Flag Warning for the affected fire-weather zones.

The NWS defines a Red Flag Warning as an alert issued "in conjunction with land management agencies, to alert land managers to an ongoing or imminent critical fire weather pattern," used when conditions are occurring or expected shortly. A Fire Weather Watch is the earlier stage, meaning critical conditions are "possible but not imminent or occurring."

In Washington those alerts come from the NWS forecast offices in Spokane, Seattle and Pendleton, and they are worth treating as a planning trigger. A live map that carries the alert polygons, as US Wildfire Tracker does, shows you the warning and the fires inside it at the same time.

Above a Red Flag Warning sits a rarer alert. DNR reported in 2026 that the National Weather Service had issued a Particularly Dangerous Situation alert for eastern Washington, "the first time a PDS has been issued for Washington state," describing it as a step above the more common Red Flag Warning.

What officials say when conditions turn extreme"With extremely dry fuels on the landscape, wildfires are already growing rapidly and are hard to control," said Russ Lane, DNR's Wildland Fire Division manager, warning that high winds would "create explosive fire growth that is rarely seen in Washington state" — DNR, July 31, 2026.

The long-term trend is toward a longer exposure window. DNR, citing US Forest Service data, notes that the average wildfire season nationally is now 78 days longer than it was in the 1970s, and that Washington homeowners are moving into the wildland urban interface faster than in any other state.

How do Level 1, 2 and 3 evacuations work?

Washington uses a three-level evacuation system rather than the Ready-Set-Go phrasing common in the Southwest. The Washington Military Department's Emergency Management Division publishes the wording, and county sheriffs apply it on the ground.

County wording also varies. Okanogan County labels the levels Alert, Be Ready and Immediate Evacuation, while King, Pierce, Snohomish and Thurston counties brand the same three levels as the regional "Ready, Set, Go!" program. The level numbers are the constant; the words around them are not.

Oregon uses the same three-level structure with different official phrasing — Be Ready, Be Set, Go Now — which is worth knowing if you travel or own property on both sides of the Columbia River.

How do you read a Washington fire map fast?

Checking Washington fire activity should take under a minute. The sequence below works on the live map or in the Chrome side panel, and it prioritizes proximity over headline acreage.

  1. Open the map and filter to Washington, or allow location access to see only fires within a set distance in miles of where you are.
  2. Read the fire probability tier first. Weak signals below 25 percent are hidden by default because they are often prescribed burns or industrial heat.
  3. Open the nearest incident and check three timestamps: last NIFC update, last satellite heat and last GOES heat. A perimeter from yesterday plus heat from 40 minutes ago is a fire still moving.
  4. Check whether a Red Flag Warning polygon covers your area, because that changes how fast the picture can change.
  5. Confirm evacuation status with your county sheriff or emergency management office, then use the directions-away links if you need to move.

What does wildfire smoke do to Washington air?

Smoke is the part of a Washington fire season that reaches people hundreds of miles from any flame. The Department of Ecology states the case bluntly: "Smoke from wildfires is the largest source of particle pollution in Washington."

The timing is predictable enough to plan around. The Department of Health advises that "Wildfire smoke season typically starts in July or August, but can begin as early as May," and recommends that everyone limit outdoor activity once air quality reaches the Unhealthy category.

Air quality is reported on the Air Quality Index. The EPA describes it as six color-coded categories, where "an AQI value of 100 generally corresponds to an ambient air concentration that equals the level of the short-term national ambient air quality standard for protection of public health."

Frequently asked questions about the Washington fire map

How often does a Washington fire map update?

It depends on the layer. Satellite thermal detections arrive several times a day from polar-orbiting instruments, with NOAA geostationary heat filling gaps between passes. Official incident records and perimeters update on agency schedules, often once per operational period. US Wildfire Tracker refreshes automatically every few minutes while it is open and shows the last-update timestamp for each source.

What does a Level 2 evacuation mean in Washington?

Washington's Emergency Management Division defines it as "get set to leave at a moment's notice." You should have an emergency kit packed, know your route and meeting place, and move livestock or large animals now. If anyone in the household has mobility or medical issues, the state advises considering leaving at Level 2 rather than waiting for Level 3.

Who decides evacuations in Washington?

Local authorities do. The state says most evacuations are decided at the local level, and the Department of Natural Resources notes that sheriff departments and local fire districts order and communicate evacuations. Firefighting agencies fight the fire; county emergency management and law enforcement move people. Always confirm with your county rather than relying on a map alone.

How many Washington wildfires are human-caused?

Most of them. The Department of Natural Resources reported that in 2025, 90 percent of all wildfires in Washington were human-caused, with lightning accounting for the remaining 10 percent. That share is why the state runs burn restrictions, Industrial Fire Precaution Levels and public-use closures during the summer rather than relying on suppression alone.

What was the largest wildfire in Washington history?

It depends on the record. The Department of Fish and Wildlife calls the 2020 Cold Springs Canyon and Pearl Hill fire, at over 410,000 acres, the largest wildfire in Washington recorded history. The 2014 Carlton Complex, at 256,108 acres, remains the largest single fire to burn entirely within the state; it destroyed 353 homes and caused roughly $98 million in damage.

Is a Red Flag Warning the same as a fire?

No. A Red Flag Warning is a National Weather Service alert about weather, issued with land management agencies when a critical fire weather pattern is occurring or expected shortly. It means new ignitions would spread dangerously, not that a fire exists. A Fire Weather Watch is the earlier stage, meaning those conditions are possible but not yet imminent.

Does the Washington fire map work west of the Cascades?

Yes. The same national feeds cover every county, including Puget Sound and the coast, and the map will show small fires in the Olympics or the southwest Washington foothills. West-side fires are less frequent, but east-wind events have produced serious ones. The map also carries smoke overlays, which matter west of the crest even when no local fire is burning.

Can I track fires in Washington and Idaho at once?

Yes. The national view shows every state at the same time, which is useful along the border with Idaho where smoke and fire activity cross state lines around Spokane and the Panhandle. You can also filter to a single state, or use the fires-near-me view, which ignores state boundaries and works on distance in miles.
