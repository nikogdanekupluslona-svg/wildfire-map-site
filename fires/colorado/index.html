What does a Colorado fire map show right now?

A Colorado fire map shows four separate things at once: incidents reported by agencies to the national interagency system, mapped fire perimeters, satellite thermal detections, and active National Weather Service fire-weather alerts. Each layer updates on its own schedule, so reading them together matters more than reading any one of them.

Incident records carry the numbers people quote: fire name, acres, percent contained, incident type and the managing organization. They are authoritative but not instant, because they change when an agency files an update rather than when the fire moves.

Satellite detections move faster. NASA's MODIS and VIIRS instruments and NOAA's GOES satellites report heat within minutes to a few hours, which is why a new start in the Colorado high country often appears as a cluster of hotspots before any agency has named it. On the live map, each detection carries a fire probability tier that blends whether an official record is linked, how fresh the perimeter is, and how recent the satellite heat is.

Read hotspots carefullyA thermal detection is a measurement of heat, not a confirmed wildfire. In Colorado the same signature can come from a prescribed burn, an agricultural pile burn on the eastern plains, or industrial heat. Treat unconfirmed hotspots as questions and official records as answers.

When is Colorado's wildfire season?

Colorado no longer has a fire season with clean edges. The state's own record makes the point: the Colorado Division of Fire Prevention and Control reports that all 20 of Colorado's largest wildfires have occurred since 2002, and the single most destructive fire in state history started on December 30, 2021.

In practice, the busiest stretch runs from late May through October, with two distinct peaks. Early summer brings wind-driven starts before the North American Monsoon arrives, and late summer brings lightning fires after dry storms cross the Western Slope.

In 2025, federal and state reporting agencies logged 1,123 wildfires in Colorado that burned 255,968 acres, according to the National Interagency Coordination Center annual report. Lightning caused fewer than half of those fires but 209,688 of the acres.

Winter and early spring are quieter but not safe. Grass on the Front Range cures over the winter, and a downslope windstorm can move fire through it at any point between October and April.

Which agencies fight wildfires in Colorado?

The Colorado Division of Fire Prevention and Control, part of the Colorado Department of Public Safety, is the state's wildland fire agency. Its Wildland Fire Management section runs state aviation and ground resources, the Colorado Wildfire Information Management System, and a weekly Fire Environment and Weather Brief prepared by its Fire Intelligence Unit.

Above the state level sits the Rocky Mountain Area Coordination Center, based in Lakewood, which mobilizes crews, aircraft and incident management teams across the geographic area and publishes the preparedness level you often see quoted during a busy week.

Federal land managers carry much of Colorado's fire load directly. In 2025 the Bureau of Land Management reported 116,568 acres burned in Colorado and the Forest Service 41,605 acres, while fires under county, state and local protection accounted for 81,784 acres.

Where do Colorado wildfires usually burn?

Colorado burns in three different landscapes, and the fires behave nothing alike. The Western Slope carries pinyon-juniper, sagebrush and mixed conifer, and produced the Pine Gulch and Lee fires. The Front Range foothills mix ponderosa pine with dense subdivisions. The eastern plains carry grass that can run for miles in a single afternoon.

That mix explains why acreage and destruction rank differently. The largest Colorado fires by acreage burned mostly in forest and rangeland, while the most destructive fires by homes lost burned where fuel met neighborhoods.

The 2025 season followed the same pattern. A dry lightning event on July 10 started the South Rim and Turner Gulch fires on the Western Slope, and the Lee Fire in Rio Blanco County grew to become the fifth-largest wildfire in state history.

Colorado's record book is recent. DFPC's historical wildfire page reports that 17 of the top 20 largest fires have occurred since 2012 and six of the top 20 since 2020, with four of the top five burning in either 2020 or 2025.

Why did the Marshall Fire burn in December?

The Marshall Fire destroyed 1,084 homes in Boulder County on December 30, 2021, in cured grass rather than timber. It is the clearest evidence that a Colorado fire map is worth checking outside summer.

No Red Flag Warning was in effect. According to NOAA Research, a warning was not issued because relative humidity did not fall below the National Weather Service's 15 percent threshold, which was established for warm-season wildfires; a high-wind warning was issued instead, and that warning triggered a county-wide burn ban.

What made the wind unusual"The measurements we've seen show that during the Marshall Fire, the wind didn't fluctuate much over the course of the event," said Paul Schlatter, science and operations officer at the local National Weather Service forecast office. "It was always out of the southwest, gusting at 60 to 100 mph for 10 to 11 hours." NOAA Research

Stan Benjamin, a CIRES scientist working at NOAA's Global Systems Laboratory and lead author of the study, told NOAA Research that the duration of the hurricane-force winds was likely the single most important factor in how the fire spread.

What weather drives Colorado fire danger?

Three ingredients drive Colorado fire danger: low relative humidity, sustained wind, and fuels dry enough for land managers to call critical. The National Weather Service issues Red Flag Warnings only when the weather and the fuels line up.

NWS Grand Junction, which covers western Colorado and part of eastern Utah, publishes its thresholds. Its primary Red Flag criteria are relative humidity of 15 percent or less combined with sustained winds or frequent gusts of 25 mph or greater, occurring simultaneously for at least three hours in a 12-hour period, or widely scattered dry thunderstorms with 15 percent or more coverage.

A Fire Weather Watch comes first, generally 24 to 72 hours ahead of expected red flag conditions, and the warning follows within 12 to 24 hours of the event. Contributing factors include the first significant lightning after a hot dry spell and dry cold-frontal passages that bring an abrupt wind shift.

How do evacuations work in Colorado?

Evacuations in Colorado are ordered by county sheriffs and local emergency managers, not by the state, and the wording has been deliberately standardized. The Colorado Division of Homeland Security and Emergency Management publishes recommended evacuation terminology built around plain language.

DHSEM recommends phrases that carry the action inside them: "Evacuation Order - Leave Now" for an immediate threat, and "Pre Evacuation Issued - If you need extra time to leave or have animals you can leave now" for a preparatory notice. The terms are best practice rather than a state requirement.

Colorado does not use Ready, Set, GoDHSEM specifically lists "Ready, Set, Go" and "Stage 1, 2 or 3 evacuation status" as terminology it does not recommend, because research shows those labels are not immediately understood and shift the work of translation onto the person receiving the alert. Do not assume the phrasing used in a neighboring state applies here.

Sign up for your county's alert system before you need it. A fire map tells you where the fire is; only your county can tell you that your address is being evacuated.

How do you read a Colorado fire map in 60 seconds?

A Colorado fire map answers one practical question fastest when you work through it in a fixed order. The sequence below takes under a minute and works during an active incident or a routine check.

  1. Open the live fire map and filter to Colorado, or allow location access to see fires near you within a set distance in miles.
  2. Look first for official perimeters. A drawn perimeter means an agency has mapped the fire and filed an incident record.
  3. Open the largest incident near you and read acres, percent contained, incident type and the managing organization.
  4. Check the timestamps: "Last NIFC update", "Last satellite heat" and "Last GOES heat" tell you how old each number really is.
  5. Scan for NWS Red Flag Warnings and Fire Weather Watches over your county, since those change how fast an existing fire can move.
  6. Check your county alert system and local news for the evacuation status of your own address.

The same routine works for neighboring states. If smoke is arriving from outside Colorado, check the Wyoming fire map and the Utah fire map before assuming the source is local, and see our guide to reading a live US wildfire map for the layer-by-layer detail.

What does wildfire smoke do to Colorado air?

Smoke is Colorado's most widely shared wildfire impact, and it routinely arrives from fires in other states. The Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment publishes a Colorado Smoke Outlook, air quality advisories and a smoke blog alongside its monitoring network.

Smoke concentrations are reported through the EPA Air Quality Index, which uses six color-coded categories. An AQI value of 100 corresponds roughly to the level of the short-term national ambient air quality standard for a pollutant.

Frequently asked questions about the Colorado fire map

How often does a Colorado fire map update?

It depends on the layer. Satellite thermal detections from VIIRS, MODIS and GOES arrive within minutes to a few hours of a satellite pass. Official NIFC incident records and perimeters update when an agency files them, which during a large Colorado incident is usually once or twice a day. US Wildfire Tracker refreshes automatically every few minutes while open and shows the time of the last update for each source.

What was the largest wildfire in Colorado history?

The Cameron Peak Fire of 2020, at 208,913 acres, according to the Colorado Division of Fire Prevention and Control. It is followed by the East Troublesome Fire at 193,812 acres, also in 2020, and the Pine Gulch Fire at 139,007 acres. All three burned in the same season, and DFPC reports that four of the state's five largest fires have burned since 2020.

Can wildfires happen in Colorado in winter?

Yes. The Marshall Fire started on December 30, 2021 and destroyed 1,084 homes, more than twice the total of any other Colorado fire. It burned in cured grass driven by sustained southwest winds gusting 60 to 100 mph for 10 to 11 hours. Cured grass and a downslope windstorm are enough; snow cover, not the calendar, is what ends the risk.

Who orders evacuations during a Colorado wildfire?

County sheriffs and local emergency managers issue evacuation orders and pre-evacuation notices, and the state supports them. The Colorado Division of Homeland Security and Emergency Management recommends plain-language wording such as "Evacuation Order - Leave Now" rather than staged labels. Sign up for your county's emergency alert system, because a map cannot tell you whether your specific address is included.

What does a Red Flag Warning mean in Colorado?

It means the National Weather Service expects critical fire weather within roughly the next 12 to 24 hours and land managers have confirmed fuels are critical. NWS Grand Junction's primary criteria are relative humidity of 15 percent or less with sustained winds or frequent gusts of 25 mph or more for at least three hours in a 12-hour period, or widely scattered dry thunderstorms.

Why does the map show heat where there is no named fire?

Satellites detect heat, not wildfires. In Colorado that heat can be a prescribed burn, an agricultural burn on the eastern plains, or an industrial source. Detections below a 25 percent probability tier are hidden by default on US Wildfire Tracker and can be shown deliberately. Treat any hotspot without a linked official record as unconfirmed until an agency names it.

Is Colorado's fire season getting longer?

The state's own records point that way. DFPC reports that all 20 of Colorado's largest wildfires have burned since 2002, that 11 of the top 20 have burned since 2018, and that the most destructive fire in state history started on December 30. The practical answer for tracking is to treat any windy, dry day with cured grass as a fire-weather day, whatever the month.

How much of Colorado's wildfire acreage is human-caused?

Less than you might expect by acreage, more than half by count. In 2025, reporting agencies recorded 578 human-caused fires and 545 lightning fires in Colorado, but lightning fires burned 209,688 acres against 46,280 acres for human-caused starts. Human ignitions are more numerous and closer to communities; lightning fires start in remote terrain and grow larger before crews reach them.
