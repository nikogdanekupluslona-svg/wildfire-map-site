What does a Missouri fire map show right now?

A Missouri fire map fuses official NIFC incident records and interagency WFIGS perimeters, NASA VIIRS and MODIS thermal detections, NOAA GOES heat detections and National Weather Service fire-weather alerts. US Wildfire Tracker renders all four free in any browser and refreshes automatically every few minutes while the tab stays open.

Missouri's signal-to-noise problem is prescribed fire. Federal and state incident systems label planned burns "RX" and wildfires "WF," and both show up as heat on the same satellite passes. The Missouri Department of Conservation and its partners prescribe burn an estimated 125,000 to 150,000 acres a year, mostly in Ozark glades and woodlands, so a February or March hot pixel in southern Missouri is often a permitted, supervised fire rather than an emergency.

US Wildfire Tracker handles that by scoring each detection: whether an official NIFC record is linked, how fresh the perimeter is, and how recent the VIIRS, MODIS or GOES heat is. Weak signals below 25 percent probability are hidden by default, and the app warns explicitly that hotspots can reflect prescribed burns or industrial heat.

2025 showed why that scoring matters at scale. Missouri recorded more than 2,700 wildfires and over 110,000 acres burned in the first ten months of the year, including roughly 9,300 acres from fires sparked by power lines, prompting the Missouri Public Service Commission to require utilities to submit wildfire mitigation plans. When is Missouri's wildfire season?

Missouri's damaging wildfires cluster in late winter and early spring, roughly February through April, when hardwood leaf litter is cured, humidity drops behind cold fronts and green-up has not yet started. Activity began rising in February 2025 and peaked catastrophically in March, according to the State Emergency Management Agency.

The defining week was March 10–16, 2025: 224 wildfires burned more than 15,500 acres statewide, most of them reported March 13–14, and more than 20 homes plus numerous vehicles and outbuildings were lost, though no injuries or deaths were reported, the Missouri Department of Conservation said. On March 14 alone, more than 100 wildfires burned simultaneously, including 21 in Boone County and roughly 30 in Camden County.

Why 2025 stood out"We are seeing a more severe, more extreme weather trend right now in Missouri that we don't see every year," said Ben Webster, MDC's fire program coordinator, describing the dry, windy pattern behind the March 2025 outbreak. Source: KCUR

The main cause is preventable: the Missouri Department of Conservation says improper burning of debris, such as trash and brush piles, is the leading cause of wildfire in the state, alongside sparks from vehicles driven through dry grass. That spring pattern is shared with the southern Plains; our Oklahoma fire map guide covers a similar late-winter grassland peak just west of the Ozarks.

Which agencies fight fires in Missouri?

The Missouri Department of Conservation is the state's lead wildland fire agency. Unlike most southeastern states, Missouri has no separate state forestry commission — MDC's own foresters and conservation agents run wildfire suppression, prevention and arson investigation statewide, and the department operates the Operation Forest Arson tip line at 800-392-1111.

MDC also funds the front line of rural response. The department supports roughly 700 mostly volunteer rural and small-town fire departments with grants for radios, chainsaws, hoses and hand tools, funded jointly by MDC and the U.S. Forest Service's Volunteer Fire Assistance Program. During the March 2025 outbreak, more than 90 specially trained MDC staff responded directly to 83 of the wildfires, covering nearly 9,000 acres.

The U.S. Forest Service manages Mark Twain National Forest, more than 3 million acres spread across southern Missouri's Ozark Highlands in six ranger districts, and coordinates suppression and prescribed fire there with state and local partners. For interagency mobilization, Missouri sits within the 20-state Eastern Area, coordinated by the Eastern Area Coordination Center rather than the Southern Area Coordination Center that covers the Deep South.

Where do Missouri wildfires usually burn?

The Ozark Highlands of southern Missouri carry the heaviest wildfire load in the state. It is oak-hickory forest broken by glades — rocky openings where bedrock breaks through thin soil — of five geological types: igneous, sandstone, chert, limestone and dolomite, according to the Missouri Department of Conservation. Chert glades are globally unique and among the state's most limited habitat.

The March 2025 outbreak showed the pattern clearly. MDC crews fought 19 wildfires covering almost 1,500 acres in the Ozark region alone, including nearly 300 acres of conservation land, while nine fires burned in southwest Missouri counties — one covering 75 acres on Comstock Prairie Conservation Area in Barton County — and MDC assisted the U.S. Forest Service on a 173-acre fire on its Seligman Hollow tract. Central Missouri's Camdenton area saw 42 fires and 6,086 acres burned, including 761 acres on Granny's Acres Conservation Area in Benton County.

Fire scar research shows this is not new. Before European settlement, fires burned every one to five years in more densely used parts of the Ozarks and every 10 to 30 years in sparser country, with major regional fire years recorded in 1728, 1753, 1772, 1780, 1795 and 1800. Fewer than 1 percent of modern Missouri fires start naturally despite 50 to 70 thunderstorm days a year — people, not lightning, have always driven Ozark fire.

Glades depend on that fire. Left unburned, eastern red cedar and other woody plants shade out the native grasses and wildflowers that support species such as collared lizards, Missouri tarantulas and Bachman's sparrows, which is why fire suppression itself now ranks among the top threats to glade habitat, alongside overgrazing and invasive plants like sericea lespedeza.

What are the largest wildfires in Missouri history?

Missouri's fire record is shaped by outbreaks of many mid-size fires rather than a single iconic mega-fire, the pattern seen in western states. The largest documented episode is the 2025 spring season, when dry, windy conditions repeatedly overwhelmed suppression resources across dozens of counties at once.

What weather drives Missouri fire danger?

The National Weather Service St. Louis office publishes explicit Red Flag Warning criteria for its Missouri coverage area: sustained 20-foot winds greater than 15 mph, relative humidity below 25 percent, and 10-hour dead fuel moisture below 9 percent, all three met at once.

March 14, 2025 showed what happens when those thresholds are badly exceeded. The NWS Springfield office recorded relative humidity below 20 percent — and below 10 percent in some areas — with 10-hour fuel moisture also under 10 percent, sustained winds of 35 to 45 mph and gusts up to 60 mph. The Storm Prediction Center issued an Extremely Critical fire-weather risk, its highest category, over the area for the first time on record.

Forecasting a moving target"We're going to have to watch out for conditions conducive for rapid fire spread," said Alex Elmore, a National Weather Service meteorologist, ahead of the March 2025 outbreak. Source: KCUR

Because Missouri is split among several National Weather Service forecast offices, including St. Louis and Springfield, a Red Flag Warning can cover one part of the state while conditions stay calmer elsewhere. Each office sets its own red flag thresholds with local fire agencies, so it is worth checking the specific warning polygon rather than assuming a statewide alert applies everywhere.

Missouri's fire signal is unusually mixed. On the same February or March afternoon, a satellite can show heat from an active wildfire in the Ozarks and heat from a permitted glade-restoration burn a few miles away, and neither looks different to a thermal sensor. That's why we treat an NIFC-linked record as the anchor and satellite heat as the supporting evidence, never the reverse. When we can't confirm which one we're looking at, we say so, and we'd rather a reader call their county emergency management office than trust a probability tier alone.

How does Missouri use prescribed fire in the Ozarks?

Missouri burns an estimated 125,000 to 150,000 acres a year through prescribed fire, according to a joint assessment by The Nature Conservancy and the Missouri Department of Conservation — and it still falls far short of what the landscape evolved with. The same assessment estimates that fully mimicking Missouri's historical fire regime would take more than 2.6 million acres burned every year.

Most of that deliberate fire goes into Ozark glades and woodlands, restoring habitat that a century of fire suppression let grow over. On the Ozark National Scenic Riverways' Thorny Creek project, the National Park Service partnered with Buffalo National River, MDC, The Nature Conservancy, AmeriCorps St. Louis and the Winona Rural Fire Department to consolidate five small burn units into one 2,232-acre unit, up from 903 acres, cutting the cost of burning from $79 to $5 an acre.

After the April 2013 burn, native grasses and wildflowers increased 120 percent in glade habitat and 56 percent in woodland habitat, both well past the project's original goals.

When prescribed fire pays offIn late February 2024, two wildfires driven by 80-degree heat burned more than 1,000 combined acres near Mark Twain National Forest's Salem Ranger District — one set by an arsonist, the other by downed power lines — yet both were contained within 48 hours. "Everything around the wildfire to the north had been prepared to be burned," said Chris Olds, the Salem District ranger, crediting earlier fuel-reduction work; a prescribed burn followed on adjacent ground that March. Source: St. Louis Public Radio

Prescribed fire also stops the moment conditions turn dangerous. Missouri has no single statewide MDC burn permit; instead, county fire departments and protection districts can declare local burn bans, and both prescribed burns and ordinary debris burning are expected to pause while one is in effect. How do evacuations work in Missouri?

Missouri does not use a statewide Ready-Set-Go or numbered evacuation-level system for wildfire. Evacuation decisions come from county sheriffs, county emergency management agencies and local fire protection districts, the same county-led pattern that handled the March 2025 outbreak across Boone, Camden, Benton, Barton and other affected counties.

The State Emergency Management Agency coordinates above that county layer. When conditions warrant it, SEMA can operate the State Emergency Operations Center and points fire victims toward the American Red Cross, which maintains six chapters across Missouri, along with the Salvation Army, the 2-1-1 helpline and Citizen Corps for food, shelter and cleanup assistance.

Fire can turn fatal fastMissouri's Division of Fire Safety notes that three people died in uncontrolled brush fires in February and March 2010 — a reminder that even routine debris burns can escalate quickly in low humidity and wind. The March 2025 outbreak, by contrast, destroyed more than 20 homes without a single reported injury or death, in large part because of rapid local response.

How do you track Missouri fires in under a minute?

The Missouri workflow centers on telling a planned glade burn from an actual wildfire, and on catching a Red Flag Warning before conditions turn as sharply as they did on March 14, 2025.

  1. Open the live fire map and filter to Missouri, or use "fires near me" to list incidents within a set distance in miles of your location.
  2. Read the probability tier first. In February and March, a low-confidence Ozark detection with no NIFC record attached is often a permitted prescribed burn rather than a wildfire.
  3. Open the fire and compare "Last NIFC update," "Last satellite heat" and "Last GOES heat." Fresh heat against a stale official record means conditions are moving faster than the paperwork.
  4. Check for an NWS Red Flag Warning polygon over the same ground, since Missouri's warnings can cover part of the state while the rest stays calmer.
  5. For evacuation and road status, contact your county sheriff's office or emergency management agency directly rather than relying on satellite data alone.
  6. Use the app's directions-away links via Google Maps, Apple Maps or Waze if you need to leave the area.

The general mechanics behind those layers, including how often each feed updates, are covered in our wildfire tracking guide. During an active outbreak like March 2025, refreshing the map every few minutes matters more in Missouri than in slower-moving western fires, since containment and new starts can both change within the hour.

What does wildfire smoke do to Missouri air quality?

Smoke from grass and timber fires can push nearby counties into worse air quality categories quickly, especially on cold, calm mornings when a temperature inversion traps smoke near the ground instead of letting it disperse. The same low-humidity, high-wind pattern that drives Missouri's Red Flag Warnings also tends to clear smoke fast once winds pick up, which is part of why conditions can shift within hours during an active outbreak.

Frequently asked questions about the Missouri fire map

How many wildfires does Missouri have each year?

Missouri recorded more than 2,700 wildfires and over 110,000 acres burned in the first ten months of 2025, an unusually active year driven by a severe March outbreak. Typical years are smaller, but the Missouri Department of Conservation says improper debris burning remains the leading cause of wildfire ignitions statewide every year.

What is Missouri's wildfire season?

Missouri's wildfire season runs roughly February through April, when cured hardwood leaf litter, low humidity and wind ahead of cold fronts combine before spring green-up adds moisture back to the landscape. The March 2025 outbreak, including a single day with more than 100 simultaneous wildfires, sits squarely inside that window.

Which agency puts out wildfires in Missouri?

The Missouri Department of Conservation is the state's lead wildland fire agency, unusual because Missouri has no separate state forestry commission. MDC also funds about 700 mostly volunteer rural fire departments with equipment grants, and the U.S. Forest Service manages suppression and prescribed fire on the more than 3 million acres of Mark Twain National Forest.

Are prescribed burns shown as wildfires on a Missouri fire map?

Federal incident systems label prescribed burns "RX" and wildfires "WF," but both produce heat that satellites detect the same way. Missouri prescribe burns an estimated 125,000 to 150,000 acres a year, so US Wildfire Tracker scores each detection by whether an official record is linked and hides weak signals below 25 percent probability by default.

Where do most Missouri wildfires happen?

The Ozark Highlands of southern Missouri carry the heaviest wildfire load, an oak-hickory forest broken by fire-dependent glades. During the March 2025 outbreak, MDC crews fought 19 wildfires covering almost 1,500 acres in the Ozark region alone, alongside large fires in southwest and central Missouri.

Does Missouri require a burn permit?

Missouri has no single statewide burn permit issued by MDC. Instead, county fire departments and protection districts can declare local burn bans, and both the Missouri Department of Conservation and the state's Division of Fire Safety advise checking with local officials before any outdoor burning, especially during dry, windy stretches.

What triggers a Red Flag Warning in Missouri?

The National Weather Service St. Louis office's published criteria require sustained 20-foot winds above 15 mph, relative humidity below 25 percent and 10-hour dead fuel moisture below 9 percent, all at once. Other Missouri-area NWS offices, including Springfield, set their own thresholds, so a warning may cover only part of the state.

Is US Wildfire Tracker an official source for Missouri fires?

No. It is a free live map built on NIFC incident records and perimeters, NASA VIIRS and MODIS detections, NOAA GOES heat detections and National Weather Service alerts, with no account required. Satellite detections can lag by minutes to hours, so follow the Missouri Department of Conservation and your county sheriff or emergency management agency for authoritative instructions.
