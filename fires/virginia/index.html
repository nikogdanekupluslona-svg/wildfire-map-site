What does a Virginia fire map show right now?

Virginia's fire count is modest by national standards — a ten-year average of about 700 fires and just under 9,500 acres — but it is unusually consistent, with two distinct seasonal peaks rather than one long fire year. More than 60 homes and other structures are damaged or destroyed in a typical year, even as suppression crews are credited with directly protecting more than 460 homes and 280 other structures worth over $60 million.

When is Virginia's wildfire season?

Virginia has two wildfire seasons, and the Department of Forestry explains why both exist: "In the spring, vegetation still dormant from the winter provides a ready fuel source. As temperatures rise, conditions become more favorable for fires to start and spread. The opposite occurs in the fall. Frost-killed vegetation and fallen leaves increase the availability of forest fuels."

The statutory spring window for Virginia's 4 PM Burning Law runs February 15 through April 30. The fall season runs roughly October 15 through November 30 — shorter than spring, but capable of producing Virginia's largest individual fires, as it did in 2016 and again in 2023.

Hurricane debris is extending the risk"Hurricane Helene, as well as recent winter storms, generated significant amounts of woody debris, which increased fuel loads and created access challenges in some areas," said DOF Chief of Fire and Emergency Response John Miller after the 2025 spring season. "Debris generated by Helene will pose a challenge for at least the next five years in southwest Virginia." Source: WRIC

Which agencies fight wildfires in Virginia?

The Virginia Department of Forestry (DOF) "is charged with the protection of Virginia's forest resources from fire," with a resource protection program built to prevent loss of life, minimize property damage and protect natural resources — every member of the agency has fire responsibilities.

On federal land, including the George Washington and Jefferson National Forests and Shenandoah National Park, the U.S. Forest Service and National Park Service manage their own suppression; Virginia's largest recent fire, the Matts Creek Fire, burned on Jefferson National Forest land under U.S. Forest Service jurisdiction rather than DOF's.

The Virginia Department of Emergency Management (VDEM), created in 1973, coordinates the broader state response when a wildfire threatens communities at scale, and interagency mobilization for major incidents runs through the Southern Area Coordination Center in Atlanta.

Where do Virginia wildfires usually burn?

Southwest and western Virginia carry most of the state's serious wildfire risk — the Blue Ridge, the Shenandoah Valley and the Appalachian counties along the Kentucky and West Virginia borders, where steep terrain, dense forest and rural homes sit close together. NWS Blacksburg's own fire-weather criteria treat the Blue Ridge as a meaningful dividing line, applying a stricter standard for counties east of it.

Central and eastern Virginia see fewer and generally smaller fires, mostly tied to debris burning on flatter terrain. Southwest Virginia carries an added burden after Hurricane Helene: downed timber across the region increased fuel loads and complicated access for crews, a challenge DOF expects to persist for years.

What are the largest wildfires in Virginia's recent history?

Virginia's largest fires cluster in the fall, on national forest and national park land where steep terrain slows containment. The Matts Creek Fire, which burned on Jefferson National Forest land in fall 2023, reached 11,016 acres — described in state reporting at the time as the largest in Virginia by far.

Virginia's own fall-season statistics show how unusual a year like 2023 or 2016 is: the state's fall fire season (October 15–November 30) burned 13,902 acres statewide in 2023 and 16,837 acres in 2016 — both roughly ten times the totals of quieter years like 2019 (242 acres) or 2021 (330 acres). The 2023 total does not include the Matts Creek Fire, since it burned on federal rather than state-protected land.

For national scale, NIFC recorded 77,850 wildfires and 5,131,474 acres across the entire United States in 2025 — Virginia's largest single fires are still small next to a Western megafire, but they are exceptional events by the state's own record.

How does Virginia's 4 PM Burning Law work?

Virginia's 4 PM Burning Law, codified at Code of Virginia Section 10.1-1142(B), makes it unlawful, each day from February 15 through April 30, to burn brush, leaves, grass, debris or dry-grass fields located within 300 feet of woodland, brushland or a field containing dry grass or other flammable material — except between 4:00 p.m. and midnight. Violating it is a Class 3 misdemeanor per offense, and a person whose fire escapes is also liable for the cost of suppression.

Why 4 p.m. specificallyDOF explains the timing: "Postponing outdoor burning until the late afternoon means decreasing potential for a fire to spread — temperature is lower, humidity is higher, and winds are typically calmer." The law is spring-only; Virginia has no equivalent statewide restriction for the fall season. Source: Virginia Department of Forestry

A separate, year-round rule in the same statute requires anyone who builds or uses an open-air fire within 150 feet of woodland, brushland or a dry-grass field to fully extinguish it before leaving and never leave it unattended — a distinct obligation from the 300-foot, February–April, 4 p.m. rule above.

What weather drives Virginia fire danger?

Three National Weather Service offices issue fire weather guidance for Virginia: Blacksburg covers the southwest and Blue Ridge, Wakefield covers the coastal plain and Hampton Roads, and Sterling covers Northern Virginia and the D.C. region.

Fuel moisture
10-hour fuel moisture of 7 percent or less.
Humidity
Relative humidity below 30 percent.
Wind
Sustained wind of 20 mph or greater.
Temperature
Above 40°F, required in addition to the above for counties east of the Blue Ridge.

Those criteria, confirmed consistently by both the Blacksburg and Wakefield offices, are meaningfully stricter on wind than many neighboring states' Red Flag thresholds — a reflection of how much Virginia's mountain terrain accelerates fire spread once wind exceeds that level.

How do evacuations work in Virginia?

Virginia does not have a statewide wildfire-specific evacuation-level system. VDEM's only named, mapped evacuation-zone program, "Know Your Zone," is a hurricane and coastal-storm tool covering roughly 1.25 million residents in Coastal Virginia — it does not apply to wildfire.

For wildfire, evacuation instructions are issued locally by county and municipal officials working with the Department of Forestry and local fire departments, based on the specific fire's behavior and terrain. County emergency management channels are the authoritative source during an active fire.

A live fire map is a situational-awareness tool for deciding whether to pay closer attention to a nearby fire — not a substitute for an official evacuation order from local authorities.

How do you read a Virginia fire map fast?

In Virginia the recurring question is whether a fire sits inside the 4 p.m. burning-law window or has escaped it, and whether it is on the steep, forested terrain where Virginia's largest fires have historically grown.

  1. Open the live fire map and filter to Virginia, or use "fires near me" to list incidents within a set distance in miles of your location.
  2. Check the probability tier first. A low-confidence detection with no NIFC record attached is often a permitted after-4 p.m. burn rather than an active wildfire.
  3. Open the fire and compare "Last NIFC update," "Last satellite heat" and "Last GOES heat" — fires on Blue Ridge and national forest terrain can outrun a stale perimeter quickly.
  4. Check acres and containment together, and note whether the fire sits in southwest or western Virginia, where most serious activity concentrates.
  5. Look for a Red Flag Warning polygon, and check the season and time of day before planning any outdoor burning of your own.
  6. For evacuation status, go to your county emergency management channel, and use the directions-away links if you need to leave.

What does wildfire smoke do to Virginia air quality?

Smoke from Virginia wildfires is measured on the same EPA Air Quality Index used nationwide. Fall fires on the Blue Ridge and in Shenandoah National Park can settle smoke into mountain valleys overnight, reducing visibility on rural roads well before regional air-quality monitors register a health warning.

FAQ about the Virginia fire map

When is Virginia's wildfire season?

Virginia has two seasons: spring, when the 4 PM Burning Law is in effect from February 15 through April 30, and fall, roughly October 15 through November 30. Spring fires happen because winter-dormant vegetation is a ready fuel source; fall fires happen because frost-killed vegetation and fallen leaves increase available fuel.

What is Virginia's 4 PM Burning Law?

Code of Virginia Section 10.1-1142(B) makes it unlawful, each day from February 15 through April 30, to burn within 300 feet of woodland, brushland or dry-grass fields except between 4:00 p.m. and midnight. Burning is postponed to the afternoon because temperature is lower, humidity is higher and winds are typically calmer by then. Violations are a Class 3 misdemeanor.

What is Virginia's largest recent wildfire?

The Matts Creek Fire, which burned 11,016 acres on Jefferson National Forest land in fall 2023, was described in state reporting as Virginia's largest by far at the time. Because it burned on federal land, it is not included in the Department of Forestry's own state wildfire totals for that year.

What causes most Virginia wildfires?

Escaped debris burning is Virginia's leading cause of wildfire, according to the Department of Forestry, which is why the agency's fire-law enforcement and prevention programs focus specifically on safe outdoor burning. Hurricane Helene debris has added extra fuel load across southwest Virginia since 2024.

What triggers a Red Flag Warning in Virginia?

NWS Blacksburg and Wakefield use consistent statewide criteria: 10-hour fuel moisture of 7 percent or less, relative humidity below 30 percent, and sustained wind of 20 mph or greater; counties east of the Blue Ridge also require a temperature above 40°F.

Is US Wildfire Tracker an official source for Virginia fires?

No. It is a free live map that fuses NIFC incident records and perimeters, NASA VIIRS and MODIS detections, NOAA GOES heat detections and National Weather Service alerts, with no account required. Satellite detections can lag by minutes to hours, so follow the Virginia Department of Forestry and your county emergency management for authoritative instructions and evacuation orders.
