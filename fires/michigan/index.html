What does a Michigan fire map show right now?

A Michigan fire map shows the same four layers used anywhere else in the country: agency incident records, mapped fire perimeters, satellite thermal detections and active National Weather Service fire-weather alerts. In Michigan, reading them together matters even more than in a big western state, because most incidents here never grow large enough to make the news.

Incident records carry the numbers people quote: fire name, acres, percent contained, incident type and the managing organization. They are filed by an agency, so they are authoritative but not instant.

Satellite detections move faster. NASA's MODIS and VIIRS instruments and NOAA's GOES satellites can report heat within minutes to a few hours, which matters most in Michigan's Upper Peninsula, where a start on remote state or federal land can burn for a while before anyone sees smoke.

On the live map, each detection carries a fire probability tier that blends whether an official record is linked, how fresh the perimeter is, and how recent the satellite heat is. Our guide to reading a live wildfire map covers what each layer means in more detail.

Read hotspots carefully in MichiganA thermal detection is a measurement of heat, not a confirmed wildfire. In a state where the most common ignition is a resident burning yard debris, an unconfirmed hotspot in spring is just as likely to be a legal debris pile as a wildfire. Treat it as a question until an agency record or a news report confirms it.

When is Michigan's wildfire season?

Michigan's wildfire season is concentrated in spring, not summer. The Michigan Department of Natural Resources reports that most of the state's wildfires occur between April and June, after the winter snowpack disappears and before new grass and leaves green up the landscape.

Dan Laux, the DNR's fire supervisor, put it plainly in an interview with Detroit public radio station WDET: "In Michigan, our fire season starts in the spring when we lose our last snow pack and before the grasses and other fuels start to green up. Everything is still pretty much dried up and brown. That's when it's most susceptible to burning. That usually runs through the end of May on average statewide."

Lightning becomes a more common cause later in the year. Laux told WDET that lightning-caused fires are "typically later in the summer," after thunderstorms move through the northern Lower Peninsula and Upper Peninsula. The 1976 Seney Fire and the 2007 Sleeper Lake Fire, two of the state's largest modern wildfires, both started from lightning strikes in late July and early August.

Scale is where Michigan differs most from western states. Local fire departments handle most wildfires that start in privately owned woods, and the DNR itself responds directly to an average of about 400 fires a year on state land or in support of local crews, according to Laux — a small fraction of the roughly 10,000 to 12,000 fire starts, most of them under an acre, that Michigan agencies collectively report fighting in a typical year.

Michigan's history complicates a simple "spring season" story. The state's deadliest fires on record did not burn in spring at all — they burned in fall, after harvest, when logging slash and cured field stubble met a dry southwest wind. That pattern is old enough that it predates the DNR's modern wildfire statistics, but it still shapes how the state thinks about fire risk outside the spring window.

Why do Michigan's jack pine barrens need fire?

Michigan's jack pine barrens are a fire-dependent ecosystem, and their most famous resident, the Kirtland's warbler, needs wildfire or its prescribed-fire substitute to keep existing. Jack pine cones are serotinous: sealed shut with resin that only the heat of fire reliably melts, releasing seed onto the bare, sun-warmed sand the young trees and nesting warblers both require.

Decades of fire suppression across northern Michigan starved that cycle. "Unless we come up with a way to put fire on the ground over large areas safely, we are going to be planting young jack pine for quite a while," Dan Kennedy, the Michigan DNR's endangered species coordinator, told Smithsonian magazine.

Christie Deloria, a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service biologist who has studied the warbler for two decades, said prescribed burning "can be used, but we have to keep the other tools in the toolbox, such as cutting trees and replanting them."

The reason agencies lean on cutting and replanting instead of fire traces back to one afternoon in Oscoda County. On May 5, 1980, Huron-Manistee National Forest crews lit the Crane Lake prescribed burn to regenerate jack pine for Kirtland's warbler habitat. Driven by wind and inexperienced crews on an unfamiliar fuel type, it escaped its control lines and became the Mack Lake Fire, burning roughly 23,834 acres, destroying 44 structures in the Mack Lake subdivision, and killing dozer operator James Swiderski.

What the Mack Lake Fire changedThe escape led federal land managers to place a two-year moratorium on prescribed burning in Michigan while training, equipment and organizational standards were rebuilt. Today, agencies manage roughly 150,000 acres of public jack pine land mainly through clear-cutting, seeding and replanting, turning to prescribed fire only where it can be used without risking life or property. Smithsonian magazine

The Kirtland's warbler recovery is one of the success stories built on that cautious approach: the 1976 federal recovery plan set a goal of 1,000 singing males a year, and counts had reached 2,365 by 2015. The bird remains, in biologists' term, "conservation reliant" — it depends on the jack pine management substituting for the fire regime Michigan no longer lets burn freely.

Which agencies fight wildfires in Michigan?

The Michigan Department of Natural Resources' Forest Resources Division is the state's wildland fire agency. It runs Michigan's burn permit system, staffs wildfire suppression crews and bulldozer plow units, and issues public fire-danger updates for state forestland, which covers a large share of the northern Lower Peninsula and Upper Peninsula.

Above the state level, the National Interagency Coordination Center's Eastern Area Coordination Center, headquartered in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, mobilizes personnel, aircraft and equipment across a twenty-state Eastern Area that includes Michigan, providing logistics, predictive services and intelligence for wildland fire activity across the region.

Locally, the Michigan Interagency Dispatch Center in Cadillac runs 24-hour dispatch for four federal partners active in the state — the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, USDA Forest Service, National Park Service and Bureau of Indian Affairs — coordinating aviation and ground resources, weather and fire-danger assessment, and incident information across Michigan's dispatch zone.

On private land, township and municipal fire departments handle the majority of first response, with the DNR called in mainly for fires on state forestland, in remote areas, or when a fire outgrows local resources.

Where do Michigan wildfires usually burn?

Michigan wildfires cluster in two regions: the jack pine plains of the northern Lower Peninsula, centered on Crawford, Oscoda, Iosco and Roscommon counties, and the forested, sparsely populated Upper Peninsula, especially Luce, Schoolcraft and Marquette counties. Both areas combine flammable conifer fuels with low population density, which lets a fire grow before it is spotted.

Recent seasons show the pattern. In 2023, the Wilderness Trail Fire burned about 3,000 acres across Crawford and Iosco counties after an unattended campfire, and the smaller Oats Fire burned in Iosco County the same week. In 2022, the lightning-caused Blue Lakes Fire burned 2,516 acres across Montmorency and Cheboygan counties. In 2021, the Brittle Fire burned 5,781 acres in Iosco County after escaping a U.S. Forest Service prescribed burn — a smaller echo of the same risk that produced the Mack Lake Fire four decades earlier.

Southern Lower Peninsula counties see far fewer wildfires. Farmland and denser development leave less continuous fuel, and it is also where the DNR's burn permit system does not apply at all — residents there check with their township or local fire department instead, a split covered in the next section.

What are the largest wildfires in Michigan history?

By modern DNR-era standards, Michigan's largest fires are still an order of magnitude smaller than the West's. The 1976 Seney Fire, sparked by dry lightning in the Seney National Wildlife Refuge, is the largest wildfire the state has recorded since agencies began tracking fires consistently, burning through the summer and into early winter across more than 72,000 acres of the Upper Peninsula's flammable, deep organic soils.

The Duck Lake Fire is the one most Michiganders remember. Lightning started it near Duck Lake in Luce County on May 23, 2012; it burned into the village of Newberry's surrounding area, destroyed 49 homes and cabins among 136 structures lost, and was declared 100 percent contained on June 15, 2012, after nearly 300 personnel and 43 miles of fire line. Michigan's governor declared a disaster in Luce and Schoolcraft counties and imposed a statewide fireworks and outdoor-burning ban across 49 counties while it burned.

None of these fires come close to Michigan's deadliest disasters, which burned before modern record-keeping and were measured in lives lost as much as acres. Gale-force winds on October 8-10, 1871, drove fires through Holland, Port Huron and Manistee that the National Weather Service credits with more than 500 deaths statewide, under the same weather system that produced the Peshtigo Fire in Wisconsin.

A decade later, on September 5, 1881, the Thumb Fire burned more than a million acres across Michigan's Thumb region in a single day and killed more than 100 people. In 1908, a fire that started near Millersburg swept east through Presque Isle County on October 15, killing at least 37 people — including several trapped in a derailed train car — and leaving roughly 700 people homeless.

What weather drives Michigan fire danger?

The National Weather Service issues Red Flag Warnings for Michigan when warm temperatures, low humidity and sustained wind combine with dry fuels to raise the risk that a new fire would spread quickly. NWS Detroit/Pontiac, which covers southeast Michigan, defines its Red Flag Warning criteria as sustained winds averaging 15 mph or greater, relative humidity of 25 percent or less, and temperatures of 75°F or higher.

Those conditions have to be either already affecting an active wildfire or expected within 24 hours to trigger a Red Flag Warning; a Fire Weather Watch covers the same combination when it is expected within 48 hours.

Michigan's fire-weather forecasting is split across several National Weather Service offices: Gaylord and Marquette cover the northern Lower Peninsula and Upper Peninsula, where most large fires have historically burned; Grand Rapids covers the west side of the Lower Peninsula; and Detroit/Pontiac covers the southeast. Each office issues its own Red Flag Warnings and Fire Weather Watches for its own counties, so the same statewide weather pattern can trigger a warning in one region and not another.

Michigan's forests add a wrinkle California and Colorado don't have: most of the state is northern hardwood and conifer forest that resists burning, but jack pine stands are, in Laux's words, "volatile fuels" that can carry a low ground fire up into a fast-moving crown fire under the right wind. That contrast is one reason the same red flag conditions produce very different fire behavior depending on which part of the state they hit.

How do burn permits and evacuations work in Michigan?

Burn permits, not evacuation levels, are the tool that governs most Michiganders' day-to-day fire risk. The DNR requires a burn permit for most outdoor burning outside city limits in the Upper Peninsula and the northern Lower Peninsula; residents check same-day status online by county or call 866-922-BURN, and the DNR can suspend permits statewide, or by county, when fire danger is high.

South of a line running roughly through Muskegon, Newaygo, Mecosta, Isabella, Midland and Bay counties, the DNR does not issue permits at all, and residents check with their township or local fire department instead.

Evacuations, when they happen, are ordered locally rather than through a statewide staged system. During the 2012 Duck Lake Fire, Luce County authorities issued a plain "evacuation order" for the area from Pike Lake east to County Road 500, coordinated with the Michigan State Police and the Michigan National Guard.

That plain-language approach, rather than a staged "Ready, Set, Go" or numbered-level system used in parts of the West, is typical of how Michigan communities communicate wildfire evacuations. County sheriffs and local emergency managers issue the orders, with the Michigan State Police's Emergency Management and Homeland Security Division coordinating state-level support.

Check your permit before you check an evacuation mapMost Michigan residents will interact with wildfire risk through a burn permit long before they ever see an evacuation notice. Verifying same-day burning status for your county takes less time than lighting a debris pile, and it is the single step Michigan's own fire officials point to most often as preventable.

How do you track Michigan fires in under a minute?

A Michigan fire map answers one practical question fastest when you work through it in a fixed order, whether you are checking a specific incident or just scanning the state during a dry spring week.

  1. Open the live fire map and filter to Michigan, or allow location access to see fires within a set distance of you in miles — useful for our fires near me guide.
  2. Look for an official perimeter first. A drawn perimeter means an agency has mapped the fire and filed an incident record with acreage and containment.
  3. Open the largest nearby incident and check acres, percent contained, incident type and the managing organization — DNR, U.S. Forest Service or another partner.
  4. Check the timestamps: "Last NIFC update," "Last satellite heat" and "Last GOES heat" tell you how current each number actually is.
  5. Scan for NWS Red Flag Warnings or Fire Weather Watches over your county, since those change how fast a small fire could grow.
  6. If you are near the Upper Peninsula or northern Lower Peninsula, check your county's same-day burn permit status before doing any outdoor burning yourself.

The same routine extends past Michigan's borders. If you're comparing how a different fire regime tracks, see the North Carolina fire map or Florida fire map, two other states where debris burning and prescribed fire, not lightning, drive most ignitions.

What does wildfire smoke do to Michigan air quality?

Wildfire smoke reaches Michigan from both in-state fires and fires burning well outside its borders, and either source is measured the same way: through the EPA's Air Quality Index. An AQI value of 100 corresponds roughly to the level of the short-term national air quality standard for a given pollutant, and the index runs through six color-coded categories.

Because most Michigan wildfires stay small, locally sourced smoke tends to be a short-lived, localized nuisance near an active fire rather than a statewide event. Smoke that blankets large parts of the state is more often carried in from elsewhere on prevailing winds, which is why an AQI spike in a Michigan city does not always mean a nearby wildfire. Frequently asked questions about the Michigan fire map

How often does a Michigan fire map update?

It depends on the layer. Satellite thermal detections from VIIRS, MODIS and GOES arrive within minutes to a few hours of a satellite pass. Official NIFC incident records update when an agency files them, which for most Michigan fires is once the DNR or a partner agency has assessed the scene. US Wildfire Tracker refreshes automatically every few minutes while open and shows the time of the last update for each source.

What was the largest wildfire in Michigan history?

Among fires tracked by modern agencies, the 1976 Seney Fire is the largest, burning more than 72,000 acres of the Upper Peninsula after a dry lightning strike. Older fires were far larger and deadlier: the 1881 Thumb Fire burned more than a million acres and killed over 100 people, and the 1871 fires around Holland, Port Huron and Manistee killed more than 500 statewide, but neither is measured by modern agency standards.

Do I need a permit to burn debris in Michigan?

In most of the Upper Peninsula and the northern Lower Peninsula, yes — the DNR requires a burn permit for outdoor burning outside city limits, checked same-day online by county or by calling 866-922-BURN. In southern Michigan, the DNR does not issue permits at all; check with your township or local fire department instead.

When is Michigan's wildfire season?

Mostly spring. The DNR reports that most Michigan wildfires occur between April and June, after snowmelt exposes dead grass and leaf litter but before green vegetation returns. Fire supervisor Dan Laux says the season "usually runs through the end of May on average statewide," with lightning-caused fires becoming more common later in summer.

Why does jack pine burn so easily in Michigan?

Jack pine cones are serotinous, sealed with resin that only fire's heat reliably melts to release seed. That trait makes jack pine stands flammable by design, and it is also why the endangered Kirtland's warbler, which nests only in young jack pine, is tied to fire. Since the 1980 Mack Lake Fire, an escaped prescribed burn that killed a firefighter, agencies have leaned on cutting and replanting jack pine over large-scale prescribed fire.

Who orders evacuations during a Michigan wildfire?

County sheriffs and local emergency managers issue evacuation orders, as Luce County did during the 2012 Duck Lake Fire, with support from the Michigan State Police's Emergency Management and Homeland Security Division. Michigan uses plain-language "evacuation order" wording rather than a staged Ready-Set-Go or numbered-level system used in some western states.

Why does the map show heat where there is no named Michigan fire?

Satellites detect heat, not confirmed wildfires. In Michigan, that heat is often a legal debris pile burning under a valid DNR permit, especially in spring. Detections below a 25 percent probability tier are hidden by default on US Wildfire Tracker and can be shown deliberately. Treat any hotspot without a linked official record as unconfirmed until an agency names it.

Is most of Michigan's wildfire acreage human-caused?

Yes, by a wide margin. DNR officials put the human-caused share at roughly 90 percent, led by escaped debris burns, burn barrels, unattended campfires and downed power lines. Lightning accounts for the remainder and is concentrated later in summer, which is also when several of Michigan's largest modern fires, including the 1976 Seney Fire, actually started.
