What does a New Mexico fire map show?

A New Mexico fire map shows four layers at once: official incident records, mapped perimeters, satellite heat detections from NASA and NOAA sensors, and National Weather Service fire-weather alerts. US Wildfire Tracker fuses them into one free view at wildfire-map.org that can be filtered to New Mexico.

Land ownership makes New Mexico unusual. A single incident can involve the US Forest Service, the Bureau of Land Management, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, the National Park Service, the US Fish and Wildlife Service and the state Forestry Division, and the managing organization shown on a fire card tells you which agency's release will carry the authoritative update.

Those averages come from the Southwest Coordination Center's historical data, which records 49,629 New Mexico wildfires and 9,192,391 acres between 1990 and 2023. The lightning share is the number that separates New Mexico from the Southern Plains: roughly half its ignitions arrive from the sky rather than from a person.

Perimeters and hotspots age differently, and the gap matters in steep Gila and Sangre de Cristo terrain where mapping flights are less frequent. Read the two timestamps on every fire card before judging a perimeter, as our fire tracker map guide explains.

When is the New Mexico wildfire season?

New Mexico's wildfire season runs from spring into early summer and typically breaks when the monsoon arrives. The Southwest Coordination Center, which covers New Mexico and Arizona for NIFC, notes that "the Southwest Geographic area often sees its fire season end by mid to late August" in a normal year.

The season's shape is driven by wind first and heat second. Spring in New Mexico brings persistent, strong westerly wind events over fuels that have not seen rain since the previous autumn, which is why April, May and June produce the state's biggest fires while July and August usually do not.

Weak monsoons stretch the season. Bladen Breitreiter, senior meteorologist with Predictive Services at the Southwest Coordination Center in Albuquerque, wrote in September 2026 that "much of the area — especially in New Mexico — held onto fire effective fuelscapes," with fuels rebounding quickly into higher percentiles and some stations exceeding record dryness for August.

Fire season no longer ends cleanlyKrista King, meteorologist with Predictive Services at the Southwest Coordination Center, wrote in the September to December 2026 seasonal outlook that persistent warmth through October and November "keep fine fuels cured and delays typical autumn moisture-driven moderation," with Energy Release Component values remaining "elevated longer than usual." Southwest Coordination Center outlooks.

Winter fires are rare but real. The Southwest Coordination Center's own record includes the Valentine Fire of February 2000, which burned 40,333 acres in the Lincoln zone, and the Grande Complex of March 2000 at 50,000 acres.

Which agencies fight New Mexico wildfires?

The state wildland fire agency is the Forestry Division of the New Mexico Energy, Minerals and Natural Resources Department, known in interagency settings as New Mexico State Forestry. It is listed as a member agency of the Southwest Coordination Center alongside the federal land agencies.

Its jurisdiction is defined by what it excludes. The State Forester's authority under the Forest Conservation Act, sections 68-2-1 to 68-2-25, and 19 NMAC 20.3 covers "non-municipal, non-federal and non-Tribal lands statewide" — which means the largest fires in New Mexico history were fought primarily by federal agencies on federal ground.

Laura McCarthy is the State Forester, working under the department's cabinet secretary. Federal partners on the Southwest Coordination Center roster include the US Forest Service, Bureau of Land Management, Bureau of Indian Affairs, National Park Service and US Fish and Wildlife Service, and the center itself sits at 333 Broadway SE in Albuquerque.

The Forestry Division also administers the Certified Prescribed Burn Manager Program, with a prescribed burn coordinator, Brian Filip, running certifications and burn plan course approvals. That programme matters for map interpretation, because certified prescribed burns produce satellite heat that is not a wildfire.

Where do New Mexico wildfires usually burn?

Two mountain systems dominate the record. The Gila National Forest in the southwest produced the Whitewater-Baldy Complex, the Silver Fire, Dry Lakes and a long tail of other large fires; the southern Sangre de Cristo range north and east of Santa Fe produced Hermit's Peak and Calf Canyon and, before them, Las Conchas.

Ponderosa pine and mixed conifer at elevation carry crown fire under wind, and both ranges sit above communities in narrow canyons. That geometry — fuel above, people below, one or two roads out — is why New Mexico's largest fires are also among its most destructive.

The eastern plains and the Pecos zone burn differently: grass and shrub fires that move fast and end fast. The Southwest Coordination Center's largest-fire list includes the Donaldson Fire at 101,563 acres in 2011 and the Stiles Complex at 67,008 acres in March 2008, both in the Pecos zone, both short-lived.

Neighbouring states share fuels and weather. Our Arizona fire map page covers the other half of the Southwest Area, the Texas fire map page covers the grass country to the east, and the national wildfire map guide explains how fuel type changes what an acreage figure means.

What are New Mexico's largest wildfires?

The Hermit's Peak and Calf Canyon Fire of 2022 is the largest wildfire in New Mexico history. FEMA describes it as burning close to 342,000 acres and more than 900 structures, and it began as two escaped US Forest Service prescribed fires that merged.

The Hermit's Peak and Calf Canyon timeline is unusually well documented because Congress legislated compensation for it. The Las Dispensas-Gallinas prescribed burn escaped on April 6, 2022; the Calf Canyon Fire, traced to a January 2022 pile burn that smoldered underground before reemerging, began April 19; the two merged on April 27, 2022.

The financial scale is without precedent for a state wildfire. Congress dedicated $5.45 billion to compensation, and as of June 23, 2026 FEMA had provided more than $3.51 billion across more than 24,502 claims. "From the start, FEMA has worked with urgency to ensure eligible survivors receive the assistance they need as quickly as possible," said Nancy Casper, interim director of operations for the Hermit's Peak/Calf Canyon Claims Office.

New Mexico taught us to put the managing organization on the front of every fire card. A Gila lightning fire run by the Forest Service, a state-protection fire on the eastern plains and a Bureau of Indian Affairs incident all update on different schedules and publish through different channels, so knowing who owns the incident tells you where the next authoritative number is coming from. We pair that with the satellite heat timestamp, because in the Sangre de Cristo canyons the perimeter is often the older of the two.

What weather drives New Mexico fire danger?

Wind is the dominant variable in New Mexico fire weather, combined with single-digit humidity and fuels that have been drying since the previous monsoon. The National Weather Service converts that combination into a Fire Weather Watch and then a Red Flag Warning, with thresholds set by each local forecast office.

How National Weather Service fire-weather products differ

The National Weather Service glossary adds that "in some states, dry lightning and unstable air are criteria" — which describes New Mexico exactly. Roughly half of all New Mexico ignitions from 1990 to 2023 were lightning-caused, and dry thunderstorms ahead of the monsoon are a recognized trigger.

Drought sits underneath all of it. Predictive Services at the Southwest Coordination Center reported an expansion of Extreme Drought and the addition of Exceptional Drought across northern New Mexico on the US Drought Monitor, with fuels "quick to rebound into the higher percentiles" even after rainfall.

How do fire restrictions work in New Mexico?

New Mexico's State Forester can impose statewide fire restrictions by order, and those orders are one of the clearest official signals that danger is rising. Order 2026-01 opened with a finding that "due to unseasonably warm temperatures, critically low humidity, high winds and abundant dry, fine fuels, wildfire danger in New Mexico is extremely high."

The order's reach follows the division's jurisdiction: smoking, fireworks, campfires, open burning and open fires are prohibited on "non-municipal, non-federal and non-Tribal lands statewide," with narrow written exceptions. Federal land agencies issue their own separate restrictions, usually in stages.

Open burning exceptions are instructive about what agencies consider safe. Under the order, irrigated cropland may be burned only when "the day is not designated a red flag day as determined by the National Weather Service and the sustained wind does not exceed 25 miles per hour in the area," among other conditions.

The economics behind prescribed fire explain why the state keeps a certified burn programme rather than banning burning outright. The Forestry Division states that prescribed burning "costs under $200 per acre," that cutting trees runs $500 to $2,000 per acre, and that suppressing wildfire costs "from $100,000 to $1 million per day, depending on the circumstance."

How do evacuations work in New Mexico?

New Mexico uses Ready, Set, Go! terminology, promoted by the state Forestry Division and managed nationally by the International Association of Fire Chiefs. The division describes it as helping residents "be Ready with preparedness understanding, be Set with situational awareness when fire threatens, and to Go, acting early when a wildfire starts."

Ready is a property condition, not an alert. The Forestry Division defines it as "maintaining an adequate defensible space around your home," clearing brush "starting in the 5-foot zone and working your way out to the 100-200-foot zone," and warns that "flying embers from a wildfire can destroy homes up to a mile away."

Set is the stage where a live map earns its place: prepare for possible evacuation before fire arrives, with a wildfire action plan and supply kits ready. Go is explicit that you should not wait — "You don't need to wait for an evacuation order. If you feel threatened, then evacuate early." Read the Ready, Set, Go! New Mexico guidance.

US Wildfire Tracker surfaces evacuation orders and warnings and road closures from official alerts where agencies publish them, and offers directions away from a fire through Google Maps, Apple Maps or Waze. It is not an official emergency service; the order from your county or the Village authority always takes precedence.

How to read a New Mexico fire map fast

Reading a New Mexico fire map quickly is a fixed sequence rather than browsing. The goal is to establish in under a minute whether fire weather is active over you, whether anything is burning nearby, and how old the newest information about it is.

  1. Open the live map and filter to New Mexico, or allow location access to list fires within a set distance in miles.
  2. Check fire-weather polygons first. A Red Flag Warning over your county changes the weight every other marker deserves.
  3. Open the strongest marker near you and read its fire probability tier, which fuses whether an official NIFC record is linked with how fresh the perimeter and satellite heat are.
  4. Note the managing organization. It tells you whether the Forest Service, BLM, BIA, Park Service or state Forestry Division will publish the next authoritative update.
  5. Compare the "Last NIFC update" and "Last satellite heat" timestamps before trusting an acreage figure in steep terrain.
  6. Leave the tab or Chrome side panel open. Data refreshes automatically every few minutes, and the last saved results appear if a refresh fails.

Weak signals below 25 percent probability are hidden by default, and that default is worth keeping in New Mexico, where certified prescribed burns and federal fuels projects generate legitimate heat detections outside fire season. Our live fire tracker guide covers these checks in depth.

What does wildfire smoke do to New Mexico air?

Smoke settles in New Mexico valleys overnight and lifts by mid-morning, which is why a community can record hazardous air at dawn and moderate air by noon during the same fire. The measure that matters is the Air Quality Index, which the EPA describes as having "six color-coded categories, each corresponding to a range of index values."

FAQ about the New Mexico fire map

What is the largest wildfire in New Mexico history?

The Hermit's Peak and Calf Canyon Fire of 2022, which FEMA describes as burning close to 342,000 acres and more than 900 structures. It began as the escaped Las Dispensas-Gallinas prescribed burn on April 6, 2022, merged with the Calf Canyon Fire on April 27, and surpassed the 2012 Whitewater-Baldy Complex at 297,845 acres.

When is New Mexico's wildfire season?

Spring into early summer, breaking when the monsoon arrives. The Southwest Coordination Center notes that the Southwest Geographic area "often sees its fire season end by mid to late August." Weak monsoons extend it: in 2026 Predictive Services reported New Mexico holding onto fire-effective fuel conditions well past the normal season end.

How many New Mexico fires are caused by lightning?

About half. Southwest Coordination Center historical data for 1990 through 2023 records 49,629 New Mexico wildfires, of which 51 percent were lightning-caused and 48 percent human-caused. By acres the split is close too, at 47 percent lightning and 51 percent human, because human ignitions cluster near roads and communities where suppression arrives faster.

Which agency runs wildfire response in New Mexico?

The Forestry Division of the New Mexico Energy, Minerals and Natural Resources Department, known interagency as New Mexico State Forestry, with Laura McCarthy as State Forester. Its authority covers non-municipal, non-federal and non-Tribal lands. Federal fires are run by the Forest Service, BLM, BIA, Park Service or Fish and Wildlife Service, coordinated through the Southwest Coordination Center in Albuquerque.

What does Ready, Set, Go mean in New Mexico?

Ready means maintaining defensible space around your home, from the 5-foot zone out to 100 to 200 feet. Set means preparing for evacuation before fire arrives, with a wildfire action plan and supply kits. Go means leaving early — the state Forestry Division states you do not need to wait for an evacuation order if you feel threatened.

Does a hotspot always mean a wildfire in New Mexico?

No. Satellite sensors detect heat, not fire type, and New Mexico has an active certified prescribed burn programme plus large federal fuels projects. A detection can be a planned burn.
