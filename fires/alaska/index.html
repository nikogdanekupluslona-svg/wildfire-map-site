What does an Alaska fire map show right now?

An Alaska fire map fuses four public feeds: official incident records and mapped perimeters from NIFC and the interagency WFIGS services, NASA satellite thermal detections from VIIRS and MODIS, NOAA GOES heat detections, and National Weather Service fire-weather alerts. US Wildfire Tracker renders all four in one free browser view.

In Alaska the balance between those layers shifts. A fire in the road system near Tok or Sterling gets a staffed incident record, personnel counts and an updated perimeter. A lightning start 90 miles from the nearest village in a limited-protection area may be surveilled rather than fought, so satellite heat is often the freshest evidence you have.

That is why the fire probability tier matters here more than almost anywhere else. US Wildfire Tracker scores each detection by whether an official NIFC record is linked, how fresh the perimeter is, and how recent the VIIRS, MODIS or GOES heat signal is. Weak signals below 25 percent probability are hidden by default and can be switched on.

The 2026 Mukluk Fire near Tok is a good example of the difference. It started August 17, 2026, grew to 7,335 acres, destroyed 50 single residences and 108 outbuildings, and carried a full incident organization with hundreds of personnel — the kind of fire with a detailed official record behind every map marker, per the Alaska Division of Forestry & Fire Protection.

When is Alaska's wildfire season?

Alaska's burn permit season runs April 1 through August 31, when permits are required statewide unless a stricter local or federal permit applies, according to the Alaska Division of Forestry & Fire Protection. Fire activity peaks sharply in June and July, when convective storms deliver most of the state's lightning.

The dates are not fixed. In spring 2025, low snow in southern Alaska pushed managers to require burn permits from March 17 instead of April 1 in some areas, the University of Alaska Fairbanks reported. In 2026 the division extended fire season in the Delta, Tok and Copper River protection areas from September 1 through 11:59 p.m. on September 14 because conditions stayed dry.

Late June is the hinge of a normal year. On June 19, 2026 alone, automated detection recorded 4,340 lightning strikes across Alaska and started a cluster of fires in the Susitna Valley. A week later the division described the state as still being "in the heart of fire season," with new Red Flag Warnings in multiple regions.

That June-July peak is the mirror image of the Southeast, where the busy months come in late winter and spring. If you compare this page with our Florida fire map guide or the North Carolina fire map guide, the seasonal curves barely overlap.

Which agencies fight wildfires in Alaska?

Two organizations carry most of the load. The Alaska Division of Forestry & Fire Protection, part of the Department of Natural Resources, provides wildland fire protection on more than 150 million acres of state, private, municipal and agreement lands. The Bureau of Land Management Alaska Fire Service covers much of the federal interior.

Coordination runs through the Alaska Interagency Coordination Center at Fort Wainwright near Fairbanks. AICC is the geographic area coordination center for the state and the focal point for initial-attack resource ordering, logistics and predictive services, with the BLM, state DNR, USDA Forest Service, National Park Service, Bureau of Indian Affairs and Fish and Wildlife Service as cooperators.

Response is not uniform across the map. The Alaska Interagency Wildland Fire Management Plan divides the state into four protection options that set the initial response before a fire ever starts, and land managers re-evaluate them annually.

To report a wildland fire in Alaska, AICC lists 1-800-237-3633. Daily incident narratives, evacuation notices and closure updates are published at akfireinfo.com by the state division and its federal partners.

Where do Alaska wildfires usually burn?

Most Alaska acreage burns in the Interior, in black spruce, shrub and grass fuels between the Alaska Range and the Brooks Range. The Mukluk Fire's own fuel description — black spruce, shrubs, grass — is the standard recipe. Roads are scarce, so smokejumpers, helitack crews, air tankers and water scoopers do work that engines do elsewhere.

Fire is not an intruder in this landscape. Without it, the division writes, organic matter accumulates, the permafrost table rises and ecosystem productivity declines; fire removes insulating organic matter, warms the soil and returns nutrients through ash and faster decomposition. That thick organic layer is also why Alaska fires smolder in duff for weeks and can hold over between weather systems.

The geography of burning is shifting. In 2022 more than a million acres burned in southwestern Alaska, a region where wildfires have historically been rare, and in 2024 fires in the Fairbanks North Star Borough forced evacuations and burned more acres than any year since 2004, according to the University of Alaska Fairbanks.

What preparedness bought in 2026"The recent fire activity demonstrated that preparedness pays off," said Norm McDonald, Deputy Director of the Alaska Division of Forestry & Fire Protection, after crews handled more than 50 new starts in a single stretch of June 2026. "We entered the season with firefighters, aviation resources, and equipment positioned where we anticipated increased fire activity." Source: akfireinfo.com

Which Alaska fire seasons burned the most acres?

Alaska keeps its record book by season rather than by single incident, because complexes merge and remote perimeters are mapped from the air. The Alaska Interagency Coordination Center's fire history chart runs back to 1939 and puts 2004 at the top with 6,590,140 acres from 701 fires.

Recent seasons have been milder by that standard: 295,122 acres in 2023, 667,656 in 2024 and 1,006,183 in 2025. Scale still matters nationally. In 2020, the worst recent year for the whole country, all 50 states together burned 10,122,336 acres, per the NIFC national table — a single Alaska season has approached two-thirds of that.

The cause split is the detail most people get wrong. Of 464 Alaska wildfires recorded between January 1 and October 10, 2025, 210 were human-caused and 251 natural — but the human fires accounted for 1,980 acres while natural ignitions accounted for 1,005,240. Lightning does not start most Alaska fires by a wide margin; it starts almost all of the big ones.

What weather drives Alaska fire danger?

Alaska fire danger is built on lightning, wind and long daylight. Convective outbreaks in June and July can put thousands of strikes on the ground in hours, and AICC runs dedicated lightning dashboards and a Fire Weather Index database alongside its seven-day fire potential outlooks.

The National Weather Service issues Red Flag Warnings and Fire Weather Watches for Alaska as it does elsewhere. Criteria are not national: the NWS coordinates a State Annual Operating Plan with each state, and red flag thresholds are set in those plans and vary from state to state, as the agency explains on its fire weather criteria page. A warning means critical conditions are occurring or expected within 24 hours; a watch covers 24 to 48 hours out.

Snowmelt timing matters as much as any single storm. Earlier snow-off in spring has been linked to larger wildfire seasons and to a lengthening fire season, according to the Alaska Fire Science Consortium report summarized by the University of Alaska Fairbanks.

A fire map that carries NWS alerts as a layer, as US Wildfire Tracker does through api.weather.gov, lets you see the warning polygon and the active heat detections in the same frame. Our general guide to wildfire tracking walks through how those alert layers are refreshed.

How do evacuations work in Alaska?

Alaska uses the three-step Ready, Set, Go framework, published by the Division of Forestry & Fire Protection with borough-specific evacuation pages for Denali, Fairbanks, Kenai, Anchorage and Mat-Su. The actual orders come from local authorities and borough emergency managers, not from the fire agencies alone.

Ready
Create defensible space: clear combustible material from around the home, keep grass mowed short, and trim trees and bushes away from the structure.
Set
Be alert. Stay current with local media, fire departments and public safety authorities, pack a go bag, and plan and practice multiple exit routes.
Go
Leave now. Take the go kit, execute the plan, and leave well before the threat reaches the neighborhood.

The 2026 Mukluk Fire shows the full cycle. Evacuation orders around Tok were lifted on the morning of Saturday, August 29, 2026, after hot spots were extinguished and unstable trees were removed within 50 feet of structures, driveways and roads. The State of Alaska also maintains a statewide wildfire evacuation map for active incidents.

Re-entry is its own hazardAfter the Mukluk Fire, the division warned returning residents about ash pits, unstable trees, weakened root systems and debris that can contain asbestos in older structures, and recommended sturdy footwear, gloves, eye protection and an N-95 mask when handling it.

How do you read an Alaska fire map fast?

Checking Alaska takes under a minute if you work the layers in order. The point is to separate a staffed, mapped incident from an unstaffed satellite hotspot before you make any travel or evacuation decision.

  1. Open the live fire map and filter to Alaska, or use "fires near me" to show incidents within a set distance in miles of your location.
  2. Read the probability tier first. High-confidence markers have a linked NIFC record; low-confidence ones are satellite-only and may be a prescribed burn or an industrial heat source.
  3. Open the fire and check three timestamps: last NIFC update, last satellite heat and last GOES heat. A stale NIFC update with fresh heat means the fire is moving faster than the paperwork.
  4. Check acres, containment and managing organization. In Alaska the managing organization tells you whether it is a state or federal protection area.
  5. Look for an NWS Red Flag Warning polygon over the same ground before you assume the fire will stay where it is.
  6. Confirm evacuation status with your borough and the daily updates on akfireinfo.com, then use the directions-away links if you need to move.
We built the Alaska view around one problem: most of the state has no road, no camera and no reporter within 50 miles of the fire. So we lean on the timestamps. If the last NIFC update is three days old and the last VIIRS pass shows fresh heat, we would rather show you both facts than average them into one confident-looking dot. Alaska is where a wildfire map earns its keep by admitting what it does not know yet.

What does wildfire smoke do to Alaska air quality?

Interior Alaska smoke episodes are severe because the same terrain that keeps fires remote also traps their smoke. AICC links directly to Alaska Department of Environmental Conservation air quality advisories plus separate Fairbanks and Anchorage advisory pages, which is a fair signal of how routine summer smoke has become.

The scale to read is the EPA Air Quality Index. An AQI value of 100 generally corresponds to the level of the short-term national ambient air quality standard for a pollutant, and the six categories below are the ones AirNow publishes.

Frequently asked questions about the Alaska fire map

Does Alaska really burn more acres than California?

In big years, yes. The AICC fire history chart records 6,590,140 acres in Alaska in 2004 and 5,111,452 in 2015. Those totals exceed the worst single-state seasons anywhere else in the country. Alaska burns huge acreage with comparatively few fires because a single Interior fire can run for hundreds of thousands of acres in country with no roads and no structures.

Why are some Alaska fires shown but not fought?

The Alaska Interagency Wildland Fire Management Plan assigns every acre a protection option. About 270.5 million acres sit in the limited option, where fire is allowed to play its natural ecological role and response is surveillance rather than suppression. A fire can be real, mapped and completely unstaffed. The map still shows it because smoke and travel impacts do not respect management boundaries.

Do I need a burn permit in Alaska?

Burn permits are required statewide from April 1 through August 31, according to the Division of Forestry & Fire Protection, unless an area is covered by a more stringent local or federal permit. Small-scale permits cover burn barrels, maintained lawns under one acre and brush piles no larger than 10 feet by 4 feet. Dates can shift: permits were required from March 17 in parts of southern Alaska in 2025.

How fresh are satellite detections in Alaska?

Satellite thermal detections can lag by minutes to hours and can miss small or smoldering fires under cloud. In Alaska that lag is often the best information available, because official perimeters for remote fires are updated on agency schedules that may be days apart. US Wildfire Tracker shows the last satellite heat and last GOES heat timestamps separately so you can judge the age of each signal.

What does Ready, Set, Go mean in Alaska?

Ready means preparing the property with defensible space. Set means being alert, packing a go bag and rehearsing exit routes as a threat develops. Go means leaving immediately, well before the fire reaches the neighborhood. The Division of Forestry & Fire Protection publishes the framework and links to evacuation information for the Denali, Fairbanks, Kenai, Anchorage and Mat-Su boroughs.

Are most Alaska wildfires human-caused?

By count it is close to an even split, and by acreage it is not close at all. Between January 1 and October 10, 2025, Alaska recorded 210 human-caused fires and 251 natural ones, but human fires burned 1,980 acres while natural ignitions burned 1,005,240 acres. Lightning is the dominant driver of large fire growth in the boreal Interior.

Is US Wildfire Tracker an official emergency service?

No. It is a free live map that fuses NIFC incident records and perimeters, NASA VIIRS and MODIS detections, NOAA GOES heat detections and National Weather Service alerts, with no account required. It is not an official emergency service, and you should always follow evacuation orders from your borough and local authorities rather than relying on any map.
