What does an Arizona fire map show now?

An Arizona fire map shows four things at once: official incident records, mapped perimeters, satellite heat detections from NASA and NOAA sensors, and National Weather Service fire-weather alerts. US Wildfire Tracker fuses them into one free view at wildfire-map.org that filters to Arizona.

Arizona has more jurisdictions per fire than almost any state. A single incident can involve the US Forest Service, the Bureau of Land Management, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, the National Park Service and the state Department of Forestry and Fire Management, so the managing organization on a fire card tells you which agency will publish the next authoritative acreage.

Southwest Coordination Center records show 89,360 Arizona wildfires and 8,485,255 acres between 1990 and 2023. Sixty percent of those ignitions were human-caused, but lightning accounted for half the acres — a reminder that the biggest Arizona fires often start where nobody is standing.

Perimeters and satellite hotspots age at different rates, and in the Mogollon Rim country and the sky islands that gap widens because mapping flights are less frequent over rough ground. Reading both timestamps is the core skill, as our fire tracker map guide sets out.

When is the Arizona wildfire season?

Arizona's wildfire season runs from spring into early summer and normally breaks when the monsoon arrives. The National Weather Service office in Flagstaff states that "the fire weather season in northern Arizona peaks near the end of June; as this is normally the warmest and driest period of the year."

The relief is specific and dated. Monsoon season "officially runs from June 15th to September 30th," but "in northern Arizona, monsoonal moisture typically does not reach the region until the first week of July." The Flagstaff office adds that "after one to two weeks of widespread rainfall throughout early to mid July, the fire threat is greatly reduced during most years."

June is the dangerous month precisely because the monsoon has not started. The Flagstaff office explains that a strong subtropical ridge over Mexico "acts to block moisture flow toward the north. This is why northern Arizona normally sees some of the hottest and driest weather of the year during June." Every one of Arizona's five largest fires on record started in May or June.

The dry-lightning windowThe start of the monsoon does not end the fire season immediately. The National Weather Service notes that "the beginning of the monsoon season continues the threat of fires as fire starts from dry lightning are common." That two-week overlap between first storms and meaningful rain is when Arizona's map fills with new lightning-caused starts. NWS Flagstaff monsoon information.

Weak monsoons stretch the danger into autumn. Krista King, meteorologist with Predictive Services at the Southwest Coordination Center, wrote in the September to December 2026 seasonal outlook that while New Mexico cools, "warm, dry conditions in Arizona keep fuels receptive."

Which agencies fight wildfires in Arizona?

The state agency is the Arizona Department of Forestry and Fire Management, usually abbreviated DFFM. Arizona's legislative budget documents describe it as "responsible for the prevention and suppression of forest fires on State Trust Land and private land outside incorporated municipalities (22 million acres total)."

DFFM also houses the Office of the State Fire Marshal, "which enforces the state fire codes and provides training and education for fire personnel and the general public." Thomas Torres is the State Forester and department director. In 2025 the department ran roughly 235 full-time equivalent positions against a total budget across all sources of about $121.9 million.

Most large Arizona fires are federal incidents. Of the 25 largest Arizona fires recorded in the interagency ICS-209 system between 1990 and 2021, the great majority were managed by the US Forest Service, with the Bureau of Land Management and the Bureau of Indian Affairs accounting for most of the rest.

Coordination runs through the Southwest Coordination Center in Albuquerque, which covers Arizona and New Mexico and lists the Arizona Dept. of Forestry and Fire Management among its member agencies alongside the BIA, BLM, National Park Service, New Mexico State Forestry, US Fish and Wildlife Service and US Forest Service.

Where do Arizona wildfires usually burn?

The White Mountains and the Mogollon Rim carry Arizona's largest fires. The Wallow Fire on the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests and the Rodeo-Chediski Fire on Fort Apache land are the two biggest in state history, and both burned in the continuous ponderosa pine belt that stretches diagonally across eastern Arizona.

Central Arizona's chaparral and desert-grassland transition around the Tonto National Forest is the second hotspot. The Cave Creek Complex, the Bush Fire, the Telegraph Fire and the Woodbury Fire all burned there, each between 120,000 and 250,000 acres, all within reach of the Phoenix metropolitan fringe.

The sky islands of southeastern Arizona are the third. Horseshoe 2, Bighorn, Aspen and Frye all burned on the Coronado National Forest, where isolated ranges rise from desert grassland and a fire that reaches the crown of one range has nowhere to spread except upward.

The Grand Canyon and the Kaibab Plateau produced Arizona's most recent megafire. The Dragon Bravo Fire ignited July 4, 2025 on the Kaibab Plateau and burned 149,399 acres, including 71,129 acres of Grand Canyon National Park land. What are the largest wildfires in Arizona?

The Wallow Fire of 2011 is the largest wildfire in Arizona history at 538,049 acres. It burned from May 29 to July 8, 2011 on the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests in the White Mountains zone and was human-caused, according to the Southwest Coordination Center's incident record.

The Wallow Fire is also the standing example of fuel treatments working under extreme conditions. The US Forest Service reported that although 38 structures burned, treatments developed with federal, state and local government and private citizens "successfully reduced fire behavior and allowed firefighters to protect thousands of structures and, in many places, halt the spread of the fire."

Dragon Bravo shows why acreage alone understates damage. It destroyed 114 buildings and outbuildings, among them the Grand Canyon Lodge building, 16 Deluxe and 64 Budget cabins and a historic linen cabin in the National Historic Landmark District, plus 24 buildings in the Headquarters Historic District. Burn severity on park land was still 68 percent low and only 1 percent high, according to the National Park Service BAER summary.

Arizona is the state where the managing agency field earns its place on the fire card. One incident can sit on Forest Service, Park Service, tribal and state ground at once, and those agencies publish acreage on different schedules through different channels. What weather drives Arizona fire danger?

Arizona fire danger is built from wind, single-digit humidity, extreme June heat and fuels that have not seen rain since the previous monsoon. The National Weather Service turns that combination into a Fire Weather Watch and then a Red Flag Warning, with thresholds set by each local forecast office rather than nationally.

How National Weather Service fire-weather products differ

The National Weather Service glossary notes that "in some states, dry lightning and unstable air are criteria," which is the Arizona case in late June and early July. Arizona forecast offices write warnings in terms of 20-foot winds, the standard wildland measurement height, rather than the 10-metre winds used in ordinary forecasts.

Drought sets the baseline the weather acts on. Predictive Services at the Southwest Coordination Center reported in September 2026 that Arizona fuels had "largely trended toward minimums" after a quiet monsoon, with only a localized area in the southeast free of drought.

How do invasive grasses change desert fire?

Arizona's Sonoran Desert did not historically carry fire, and an invasive grass has changed that. Buffelgrass, brought from Africa and introduced near Tucson in the late 1800s for erosion control and cattle feed, now fills the bare ground between desert plants and provides a continuous fuel layer where none existed.

The heat it releases is the problem. "Buffelgrass fires can burn at over 1000-degrees Fahrenheit," said Frankie Foley, a National Park Service biologist at Saguaro National Park. "That can have a detrimental impact on the very slow growing cacti species and wildlife that are special to Saguaro National Park."

Saguaro cactus did not evolve with fire and does not survive it, so a buffelgrass fire converts desert to grassland permanently rather than resetting it. Foley notes that "buffelgrass has invaded sections of the park as well as other federally managed lands," and the Park Service treats it as the primary threat to the park's native vegetation.

For map readers, this changes where to expect fire. Low-elevation desert around Tucson and Phoenix that would once have been a natural fire break can now carry a fast grass fire in spring, which is why detections in desert terrain deserve the same attention as detections in the pines. How do evacuations work in Arizona?

Arizona uses Ready, Set, Go! terminology, and it is the sheriffs who run it. The programme has been adopted by all 15 Arizona county sheriffs, so the status attached to your neighbourhood during a wildfire comes from the county sheriff's office rather than from a fire agency.

The three levels are distinct. Ready means "prepare now" and is a property and planning condition. Set means "be alert. Know there is significant danger in your area," with essentials packed and a readiness to leave at short notice. Go means "evacuate. Danger in your area is imminent and life-threatening."

Because status is set by county, a single fire can hold neighbouring communities at different levels at the same time — one canyon at SET while the drainage beside it is at GO. Watching only the fire perimeter will not tell you that; the county's own alert channel will.

US Wildfire Tracker surfaces evacuation orders and warnings and road closures from official alerts where agencies publish them, and offers directions away from a fire through Google Maps, Apple Maps or Waze. It is not an official emergency service; the sheriff's order always takes precedence.

How to read an Arizona fire map in a minute

Reading an Arizona fire map quickly is a fixed sequence, not browsing. The goal is to establish in under a minute whether fire weather is active over you, whether anything is burning nearby, and how old the newest information about it is.

  1. Open the live map and filter to Arizona, or allow location access to list fires within a set distance in miles.
  2. Check fire-weather polygons first. A Red Flag Warning over your county changes the weight every other marker deserves.
  3. Open the strongest marker near you and read its fire probability tier, which fuses whether an official NIFC record is linked with how fresh the perimeter and satellite heat are.
  4. Note the managing organization, because it tells you whether the Forest Service, BLM, BIA, Park Service or DFFM will publish the next authoritative figure.
  5. Compare the "Last NIFC update" and "Last satellite heat" timestamps before trusting an acreage number in rough terrain.
  6. Leave the tab or Chrome side panel open. Data refreshes automatically every few minutes, and the last saved results appear if a refresh fails.

Weak signals below 25 percent probability are hidden by default, and Arizona gives that setting plenty of work: prescribed burns, pile burning on the national forests and mining and industrial heat all register on thermal sensors. Our live fire tracker guide covers the same checks in more depth.

What does wildfire smoke do to Arizona air?

Smoke from Rim Country fires drains into the Verde and Salt river valleys overnight and can reach the Phoenix and Tucson basins, where it mixes with existing summer ozone. The measure that matters is the Air Quality Index, which the EPA describes as having "six color-coded categories, each corresponding to a range of index values."

Frequently asked questions about the Arizona fire map

What is the largest wildfire in Arizona history?

The Wallow Fire of 2011, at 538,049 acres. It burned from May 29 to July 8, 2011 on the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests in eastern Arizona and was human-caused. The 2002 Rodeo-Chediski Fire, at 468,638 acres on Fort Apache land managed by the Bureau of Indian Affairs, is second in the interagency record.

When is Arizona's wildfire season?

Spring through early summer, peaking in June. The National Weather Service office in Flagstaff states that the fire weather season in northern Arizona "peaks near the end of June; as this is normally the warmest and driest period of the year," and that after one to two weeks of monsoon rainfall in early to mid July, "the fire threat is greatly reduced during most years."

Does the monsoon end Arizona's fire season?

Usually, but not instantly. Monsoon season officially runs June 15 to September 30, and moisture typically does not reach northern Arizona until the first week of July. The National Weather Service notes that the start of the monsoon "continues the threat of fires as fire starts from dry lightning are common" until sustained rainfall arrives.

Which agency fights wildfires in Arizona?

The Arizona Department of Forestry and Fire Management protects State Trust Land and private land outside incorporated municipalities, about 22 million acres, and houses the Office of the State Fire Marshal. Most large fires are federal incidents run by the Forest Service, BLM, Bureau of Indian Affairs or National Park Service, coordinated through the Southwest Coordination Center.

What do Ready, Set and Go mean in Arizona?

Ready means prepare now, long before a threat arrives. Set means be alert and know there is significant danger in your area, with essentials packed. Go means evacuate, because danger is imminent and life-threatening. All 15 Arizona county sheriffs have adopted the programme, so your status comes from the county sheriff's office.

Can the Sonoran Desert burn?

It can now. Invasive buffelgrass fills the bare ground between desert plants and carries fire across terrain that was once a natural break. National Park Service biologist Frankie Foley states that buffelgrass fires "can burn at over 1000-degrees Fahrenheit," hot enough to kill saguaro cactus, which did not evolve with fire and does not recover from it.
