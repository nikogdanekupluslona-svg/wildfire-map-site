How US Wildfire Tracker is built: every data source, its refresh rate, and what it cannot see

📅 September 2026 ⏱ 6 min read ✍ US Wildfire Tracker team

US Wildfire Tracker produces no fire data of its own. It fetches four public agency feeds, matches satellite heat detections against official incident records, and shows the result on one map. This page states exactly which feeds those are, how current each one is, and where the method breaks down.

Which data sources does the map use?

Four feeds, each published by a US federal agency and each free to access. Nothing on the map comes from a private data broker, a crowdsourced report or a scraped web page.

Two consequences follow from that table and they explain most of what confuses people about live fire maps. Satellite heat is almost always fresher than the official record beside it. And a mapped perimeter describes where a fire had reached when someone last mapped it, not where its edge is now.

Why we show three timestamps per fireEvery fire panel labels its last incident update, its last satellite heat and its last geostationary heat separately. A fire whose perimeter is two days old but whose satellite heat is twenty minutes old is behaving very differently from one where both are stale.

How are satellite detections matched to fires?

A satellite detection is a point of heat, not a fire with a name. Turning one into the other is a matching problem, and it is where a fused map either earns its keep or misleads.

When a detection falls inside or near a current official perimeter, or near a reported incident point, it is attached to that incident and inherits its name, acreage and containment. When no official record is nearby, the detection stands alone as an unnamed heat detection. We do not name it, guess its size or estimate its containment.

Clusters of detections in the same place across successive passes are grouped so that one fire does not appear as fifty separate dots. The grouping is spatial and temporal; it does not assert that the grouped points are a single incident when the agencies have not said so.

How is the fire probability score calculated?

The score is a confidence indicator, not a physical measurement, and it exists because raw satellite heat produces false positives that alarm people unnecessarily. It combines three signals.

Official corroboration
Whether a current interagency incident record is linked to the detection. This is the strongest signal.
Perimeter freshness
How recently the mapped perimeter for the linked incident was updated. A perimeter refreshed today weighs more than one from last week.
Heat recency
How recently VIIRS, MODIS or GOES saw heat at that location. Heat from the last hour weighs more than heat from yesterday.

Detections scoring below 25 percent are hidden by default and can be shown with the weak-signals toggle. Records whose perimeter and heat are both stale are labelled outdated and are also hidden by default. Both filters are yours to switch off; nothing is deleted, only de-emphasised.

What a low score usually meansA single hot pixel with nothing corroborating it is more often a prescribed burn, an agricultural fire, a flare stack, a steel mill or a solar array than a wildfire. That is why weak signals are hidden rather than shown as fires.

What can this wildfire map not see?

Stating the limits plainly matters more than the feature list, because acting on a map that seems to show nothing is the dangerous case.

  • Cloud and thick smoke block satellites. Both MODIS and VIIRS look down through the atmosphere. Heavy cloud cover can hide an active fire for an entire pass.
  • Small and smouldering fires fall below the threshold. A fire has to be hot enough and large enough within a pixel to register. Early-stage fires and creeping ground fires often do not.
  • Polar-orbiting satellites have gaps. Between passes there are hours when only the geostationary GOES view is available, at coarser resolution.
  • Official records lag ignition. A fire can burn for a considerable time before it appears in an interagency feed with a name and an acreage.
  • Perimeters are snapshots. They are mapped by aircraft, ground crews or satellite analysis at a point in time, not continuously.
  • Alert shapes are zones, not fire shapes. A Red Flag Warning covers a forecast zone. Its boundary says nothing about where a fire is.
This is not an emergency serviceUS Wildfire Tracker is an informational map built on public data. It has no role in dispatch, evacuation or official notification. If your local authorities issue an evacuation order, follow it immediately regardless of what any map shows.

What happens to your location data?

Location is requested only when you ask to see fires near you, and it is used only to sort fires by distance from you. It is never stored on a server, never sold and never shared with a third party. Declining location access costs you only the proximity ranking; the state filter and the national view work exactly the same.

The Chrome extension carries no third-party advertising trackers, and no account is needed for any feature of the map.

How are errors corrected?

Errors come in two kinds and they are fixed differently. If the mistake is ours, in how a detection was matched, labelled or displayed, we correct it and note the change here. If the mistake is in an agency feed, in an acreage or a containment figure, we cannot alter it, because rewriting an official record would make the map less trustworthy rather than more.

Report anything that looks wrong through the Chrome Web Store listing, naming the fire and what you expected to see. Include the timestamps shown on the fire panel; they tell us which feed the problem came from.

We built this map because the public data is excellent and almost nobody explains it. Our job is not to have an opinion about a fire, it is to put the agencies' own records and the satellites' own detections side by side, timestamp both honestly, and get out of the way. When the data is thin, we would rather show you that it is thin than dress it up.

Frequently asked questions about our method

Who runs US Wildfire Tracker?

A small independent team that builds and maintains the map and the Chrome extension. We are not a government agency and we are not affiliated with one. All incident data belongs to the agencies that publish it.

Do you produce any of the fire data yourselves?

No. Every fire on the map comes from a public agency feed. We fetch those feeds, match satellite detections to official incident records where we can, and present the result on one map. We do not create incidents, edit acreage, or adjust containment figures.

How accurate is the fire probability score?

It is a confidence indicator, not a measurement. It weighs whether an official incident record is linked to a detection, how recent the mapped perimeter is, and how fresh the satellite heat is. A high score means several independent sources agree. A low score usually means a single satellite pixel with nothing corroborating it.

Why does a fire sometimes appear on the map after it is out?

Incident records stay in the agency feeds until the agency closes them, and a mapped perimeter does not disappear when a fire stops growing. The map labels stale records and hides them by default, but the underlying delay is in the source data, not in the map.

Can this map miss a fire?

Yes. Satellites cannot see through thick cloud or smoke, small and smouldering fires can fall below the detection threshold, and a new fire may not appear in an incident feed for some time after it starts. Never treat an empty map as proof that nothing is burning near you.

How do I report an error on the map?

Leave a review or support message on the Chrome Web Store listing with the fire name and what looks wrong. If the error is in an agency feed we will say so, because we cannot correct another organisation's data. If the error is ours, we fix it and note it here.

See the method in action

Open the map, pick any active fire, and read its three timestamps. That single habit tells you more than any headline acreage figure.
