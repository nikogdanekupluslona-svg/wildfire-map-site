What does a fire tracker near me actually show?

A fire tracker near me shows four separate kinds of information stacked on one map: official incident records from federal and state agencies, mapped fire perimeters, satellite heat detections, and National Weather Service fire-weather alerts. Each layer has a different origin, a different update schedule, and a different meaning for your household.

Confusing those four layers is the single most common mistake readers make. An official incident record is a human-created entry in an interagency database. A satellite hotspot is an algorithm flagging a pixel that looked unusually hot during one overpass. They are not the same claim, and they do not deserve the same level of trust.

The National Interagency Fire Center is the clearing house for the official half of that picture. Its National Fire News report for September 9, 2026 listed 59 large fires under active management with 14,405 firefighters and support personnel assigned nationwide, and the national preparedness level sitting at 3 on a scale of 1 to 5.

The four data layers behind every nearby-fire answer

Official incident records carry the fields you actually make decisions on: incident name, acres, percent contained, discovery date, managing organization and personnel counts. These come from agency dispatch and reporting systems and refresh on agency schedules, which means hours rather than minutes during a busy afternoon.

Perimeters are polygons drawn from infrared mapping flights, GPS tracks walked or flown around the fire edge, and satellite analysis. A perimeter is the most trustworthy answer to "how big is it and which way has it moved," but it is also the slowest layer, because somebody has to fly or walk it before it can be published.

Satellite thermal detections come from NASA and NOAA instruments. The VIIRS 375 m active fire product flies on Suomi NPP, NOAA-20 and NOAA-21; NASA states the data are globally available within three hours of satellite observation, and that United States and Canada detections are delivered in real time. That is fast, but it is still a snapshot from a passing orbit.

Fire-weather alerts come from the National Weather Service through api.weather.gov. A Red Flag Warning does not mean a fire exists. It means the atmosphere is primed so that any ignition, including one three counties away, can run hard. Reading it as a fire report is a category error worth avoiding.

Why one fused view beats four separate lookups

Checking four agency sites in sequence takes ten minutes and produces four partial answers, which is the whole reason a fire tracker near me exists as one view. US Wildfire Tracker fuses NIFC incident records and WFIGS perimeters, NASA MODIS and VIIRS detections, NOAA Hazard Mapping System GOES heat, and NWS fire-weather alerts into a single map you can read in one pass.

The fusion matters most when the layers disagree. A hotspot with no linked incident record deserves suspicion. An incident record whose perimeter has not moved in three days but whose satellite heat is four hours old deserves attention. Seeing both timestamps side by side is what converts raw data into a decision.

Each detection in US Wildfire Tracker carries a fire probability score. The tier blends three things: whether an official NIFC record is linked to the detection, how fresh the NIFC perimeter is, and how recent the VIIRS, MODIS or GOES heat signal is. Weak signals below 25 percent probability are hidden by default, and you can switch them on deliberately.

How do I find fires near me in under 60 seconds?

Finding fires near me takes six steps and about a minute: open the live map, allow location, set a radius in miles, read the probability tier, open the nearest incident, and check for an active National Weather Service fire-weather alert over your area. Do them in that order every time.

The order is not arbitrary. Location first gives the map something to sort by. Radius second removes the visual noise of a national view. Probability third stops you reacting to an industrial heat source. Only then does reading incident detail become worth your attention.

  1. Open the live map at wildfire-map.org in any browser, or open the US Wildfire Tracker side panel if you have the Chrome extension pinned.
  2. Allow the browser location prompt. The app uses geolocation only to sort and filter fires by distance from you; it does not store, sell or share your position.
  3. Switch to the "fires near me" view and choose a search distance in miles. Start at 25 miles for a first look, then widen to 50 miles if smoke is visible but nothing appears close.
  4. Read the fire probability tier on each nearby detection before anything else. Weak signals below 25 percent are hidden by default; leave them hidden on a first pass.
  5. Open the nearest high-probability detection and read four fields in order: acres, percent contained, "Last NIFC update" and "Last satellite heat". A stale NIFC timestamp with fresh satellite heat means the picture is moving faster than the paperwork.
  6. Check whether a National Weather Service Red Flag Warning or Fire Weather Watch covers your county, because that single fact changes how much margin you should give any nearby ignition.
  7. If anything is inside 25 miles and upwind of you, use the directions-away-from-the-fire link and confirm two routes out before you do anything else.

Most people finish that loop in well under a minute after the first attempt. The habit that separates a useful fire tracker near me check from an anxious one is doing all six steps rather than stopping at step three because the map looked reassuring.

What to do when the map shows nothing near you

An empty radius is a real answer, but it is a conditional one. Satellite instruments can miss small or smoldering fires, and cloud cover blocks thermal detection outright. If you can smell smoke and the map is empty at 25 miles, widen to 50 and then check the smoke layer rather than concluding nothing is burning.

Structure fires, vehicle fires and controlled agricultural burns rarely appear at all. They are too small or too short-lived for a 375 m satellite pixel, and they never generate an interagency wildland incident record. A quiet wildfire map is not evidence that your neighbor's shed is fine.

Set the radius to your terrain, not your comfortFlat, open country with wind gives a fire far more reach in an hour than steep timbered ground with no wind. If you live on a ridge above a grass valley, treat 50 miles as your default radius rather than 25, and re-check more often on gusty afternoons.

The reverse case matters too. A single detection at 40 miles in still, humid conditions is not a reason to pack the car. Distance without wind direction and fuel type is a number without meaning, which is why the sections below build the interpretation layer around it.

Is there a fire near me right now, or just smoke?

Smoke and fire are separate questions with separate answers. Smoke you can see or smell frequently originates hundreds of miles away, while the nearest actual flame front may be in another state. NOAA describes wildfire smoke travelling hundreds, even thousands, of miles from its source before it settles into a populated basin.

The June 2023 Canadian smoke episode is the clearest demonstration on record. NOAA reported that more than 86 million people experienced fine particulate pollution above the federal health standard, and more than 17 million people in New York City, Philadelphia and Washington, D.C. were breathing air in the Very Unhealthy category.

None of those tens of millions had a fire near them. They had somebody else's fire, transported by upper-level winds and mixed down to street level. That distinction is exactly what a fire tracker near me is for: separating the hazard you must evacuate from the hazard you must filter.

How to tell local smoke from imported smoke

Three signals distinguish them. Local smoke usually arrives with visible ash or embers, a sharp resinous smell and rapidly changing visibility. Imported smoke arrives as a flat gray or orange haze that fills the whole sky evenly, smells faintly acrid, and changes slowly over hours.

The map settles it. If your radius is empty at 50 miles and the regional smoke overlay shows a plume arriving from the north, you are downwind of distant fires. If a high-probability detection sits 12 miles upwind and the haze is thickening by the hour, you are dealing with something local.

Why satellite smoke tracking gives forecasters lead timeJoel Dreessen, an air quality forecaster with the Maryland Department of the Environment, said satellite resources provide "operationally critical, real-time information on smoke content in the atmosphere and vital lead time for us to communicate impending impacts to surface air quality" (NOAA NESDIS).

The health hazard in both cases is the same particle. EPA defines PM2.5 as fine inhalable particles with diameters generally 2.5 micrometers and smaller, and notes that an average human hair is about 70 micrometers across, roughly 30 times wider than the largest fine particle. Those particles reach deep lung tissue.

Which distance from a fire near me actually matters?

Distance alone never answers the question, and a fire tracker near me will not answer it for you either. What matters is distance combined with wind direction, fuel type, terrain slope and time of day. A fire 25 miles upwind in cured grass under a 40 mph gradient wind is a more urgent problem than a fire two miles away backing downhill into wet timber.

Still, radius bands are useful because they map cleanly to different actions. The bands below are a planning convention rather than an official standard, so treat them as a way to organize your own thinking and always defer to the evacuation levels your county publishes.

The four radius bands and what each one should trigger

Inside five miles, you are in the zone where a wind shift can put you under direct ember attack within an hour. This is the band where you stop monitoring and start acting: vehicles pointed out, go-bags in the trunk, phones charged, and everyone in the household told where you will meet.

Between five and ten miles, spotting is a realistic concern under wind. NIST's reconstruction of the 2018 Camp Fire documented long-range spotting up to 6.3 kilometers, about 3.9 miles, ahead of the main front, under winds reaching 22 meters per second, roughly 50 miles per hour.

Between ten and twenty-five miles, you are in the monitoring band. Check the fire's growth every hour or two, note whether the perimeter is expanding toward you, and confirm that your two planned routes out are still open. Nothing needs to be loaded yet, but nothing should be missing either.

Beyond twenty-five miles, the practical concerns become smoke, road closures and grid reliability rather than flame. This is also the band where a fire tracker near me earns its keep for travel planning, because a fire 60 miles from you can still close the only highway through a canyon.

Why upwind distance counts double

A fire upwind of you is closing the gap at the wind's pace plus its own spread rate. A fire downwind is moving away and shrinking your exposure by the hour. Same mileage on the map, opposite meaning on the ground, which is why wind direction belongs in the first thirty seconds of every check.

The National Weather Service defines the conditions that make upwind distance collapse fastest. Its glossary sets Red Flag criteria at a sustained wind average of 15 mph or greater, relative humidity at or below 25 percent, and a temperature above 75 degrees Fahrenheit, with National Fire Danger Rating System values already high to extreme.

Those thresholds are modest numbers. Fifteen miles per hour is an ordinary breeze. The reason they matter is that they describe the atmosphere's willingness to carry fire, not the fire's own intensity, and that willingness is what turns a 12-mile buffer into a 4-mile buffer over an afternoon.

When wind events exceed the normal rangeAriel Cohen, chief meteorologist for the National Weather Service Los Angeles/Oxnard office, told NPR that typical Santa Ana winds run "on the order of, say, 30 to 60 mph when they occur, a couple times a year," and that the January 2025 event produced "a widespread, life-threatening and destructive windstorm" (NPR).

During that January 2025 event the forecast called for widespread gusts of 35 to 55 mph, strengthening by another 10 to 15 mph overnight, with peak gusts reaching 100 mph in some places. Under those numbers, radius bands compress and the only safe assumption is the pessimistic one.

Terrain multipliers most readers underestimate

Fire moves uphill far faster than across flat ground, because rising heat preheats the fuel above it. If a detection sits at the bottom of the slope your house is on, subtract mentally from the mapped distance. If it sits above you on the same slope, the fire is working against gravity and you have more time.

Fuel type changes the arithmetic just as sharply. Forest Service research found that grassland and shrubland fires accounted for 63.7 percent of buildings destroyed between 2000 and 2013, compared with 33.1 percent for forest fires. Grass carries fire fastest and is the fuel most people discount because it looks harmless.

Narrow canyons, saddles and chimneys concentrate wind and accelerate spread through them. If your route out runs through one, the useful distance is not the straight line from the fire to your house but the straight line from the fire to the pinch point on your escape road.

How accurate is a fire tracker near me?

A fire tracker near me is accurate about the existence and rough location of significant fires, moderately accurate about size and containment, and deliberately conservative about anything small. The honest framing is that it reduces uncertainty rather than eliminating it, and every timestamp on the screen exists to tell you how much uncertainty remains.

The strongest layer is the official incident record. When NIFC and its partner agencies publish acres, containment and personnel, those figures come from people standing on the fire. They are late but reliable. The weakest layer is an unlinked satellite pixel, which is fast but tells you only that something was hot.

Known limits worth stating plainly

Satellite detections lag by minutes to hours depending on orbit and processing. NASA's VIIRS product is delivered in real time for the United States and Canada and within three hours globally, but that clock starts at the moment the satellite passed overhead, not at the moment the fire started.

Small fires can be missed entirely. A 375 meter pixel averages heat across roughly 35 acres of ground; a quarter-acre smoldering fire under a forest canopy may never raise that pixel above the detection threshold. Cloud, dense smoke and heavy canopy all suppress detection further.

False positives run the other way. Industrial heat sources, flares, steel mills, solar arrays and legitimate prescribed burns all radiate in the same infrared bands as wildfire. US Wildfire Tracker warns about this directly, which is the reason the probability tier exists rather than a simple dot on a map.

A fire tracker near me is not an emergency serviceUS Wildfire Tracker is an informational map built on public agency feeds. It does not dispatch resources, it does not issue evacuation orders, and it cannot see a fire an agency has not reported and a satellite has not caught. When a local order conflicts with anything on the screen, follow the order.

How to judge a detection's reliability in ten seconds

Three fields settle it. First, is an official NIFC record linked to this detection. Second, how old is "Last NIFC update". Third, how old is "Last satellite heat" or "Last GOES heat". A linked record with both timestamps under six hours is about as solid as public wildfire data gets.

A detection with no linked record and a single satellite hit is a lead, not a fact. It might be a genuine new start that no agency has entered yet, which is precisely why the layer is valuable. It might equally be a mill. Treat it as a prompt to look outside and check local alerts, not as confirmation.

How do alerts warn me about fires near me?

Three alerting systems can reach you about fires near me, alongside the fire tracker near me view itself, and they work on completely different principles. Wireless Emergency Alerts push to phones inside a geographic polygon. County opt-in systems call, text or email addresses you registered. Proximity notifications inside a fire tracker near me watch the data feed and tell you when something new appears near your location.

None of the three is sufficient alone. WEA is the loudest but the coarsest. Opt-in systems are precise but require you to have signed up, often through a county page you have never visited. In-app proximity alerts are fast but depend on the underlying data being published in the first place.

What Wireless Emergency Alerts can and cannot do

Ready.gov describes WEA messages as looking like text messages but designed to get your attention with a unique sound and vibration repeated twice. They are not affected by network congestion, they cost nothing, and there is no need to subscribe. Authorized senders include state, local, tribal and territorial officials and the National Weather Service.

FEMA states the system's maximum character count was increased from 90 to 360 characters, and that participating carriers must deliver alerts to 100 percent of the targeted area with no more than one-tenth of a mile of overshoot, which is 528 feet. WEA rides on cell broadcast rather than ordinary text messaging.

The practical limits follow from that design. WEA reaches the phones physically present in the polygon at the moment of broadcast, so a phone that was switched off or out of coverage may never receive it. And nothing is sent until a human authority decides to send it, which is often after the fire has been running for a while.

Layering county alerts and in-app proximity notifications

Ready.gov advises signing up for community alerts in your area and being aware of the Emergency Alert System. That signup is the step most households skip, and it is the one that delivers street-level detail such as which zone is under order and where the evacuation center opened.

Smoke and air-quality overlays sit alongside them so the two hazards stay visually separate.

NOAA Weather Radio remains the quiet fourth channel. A battery receiver works when the cell network does not.

What do Ready, Set and Go mean for fires near me?

Ready, Set, Go is the three-stage evacuation language most western states use, and each stage has an official meaning tied to a specific action. Oregon publishes the clearest version: Level 1 is BE READY and colored green, Level 2 is BE SET and colored yellow, and Level 3 is GO NOW and colored red.

Learning the wording matters because the levels arrive as a phrase in an alert rather than as a layer in a fire tracker near me, often with no explanation attached. If "Level 2" means nothing to you at 4 a.m., you lose the ten minutes that the level was designed to buy you.

Level 1, BE READY

Oregon's guidance describes Level 1 as being aware of danger in the area and staying informed, packing an emergency go-kit, planning evacuation routes and considering leaving early if mobility is limited. It is the stage where preparation is still cheap and unhurried.

Oregon's Department of Emergency Management frames Level 1 as the time to pack the go-kit, check emergency contacts and make sure people in vulnerable situations, including older adults, children, people with disabilities and livestock owners, are ready to move if needed. Livestock in particular cannot be loaded at Level 3.

Level 2, BE SET

Level 2 means significant danger in the area and being prepared to leave at a moment's notice. Oregon's official language is explicit that this is the point at which voluntary evacuation is encouraged, especially for anyone who needs extra time to relocate safely.

The state's guidance adds the sentence most worth internalizing: at Level 2 there may not be enough time to pack your belongings, and doing so is at your own risk. If you have a large family, animals, or anyone who moves slowly, Level 2 is your actual departure cue, not Level 3.

Level 3, GO NOW

Level 3 means leave immediately. Oregon's wording is that there is extreme danger in the area, that it is unsafe to stay, and that staying threatens the safety of you, your loved ones and emergency responders. Do not stop to gather belongings or make efforts to protect your home.

Ready.gov reinforces the same point from the federal side: evacuate immediately if authorities tell you to do so, and if you become trapped, call 9-1-1 and give your location. Its wildfire messaging states plainly that late decisions to evacuate can lead to wildfire fatalities.

What goes in a go-bag when a fire is near me?

A go-bag is the kit you can carry to the car in one trip without thinking, and it is what a fire tracker near me is ultimately buying you time to reach. Ready.gov's basic disaster supplies list is the federal baseline: one gallon of water per person per day for several days, a several-day supply of non-perishable food, a first aid kit, flashlight, extra batteries and a manual can opener.

The federal list also names items households routinely forget: a battery-powered or hand-crank radio with NOAA Weather Radio tone alert, a whistle for signaling help, a dust mask, plastic sheeting and duct tape, moist towelettes and garbage bags, a wrench or pliers to shut off utilities, local paper maps, and a cell phone with chargers and a backup battery.

For wildfire specifically, Ready.gov adds two items that do not appear on the generic list. Store an N95 mask to protect yourself from smoke inhalation, and keep your cell phone charged when wildfires could be in your area. Both are trivial to arrange and disproportionately useful.

The Five Ps, and why they beat a long list under pressure

Ready.gov's wildfire messaging condenses the whole problem into the Five Ps of Evacuation: People, Prescriptions, Papers, Personal Needs and Priceless Items. Under stress, a five-word mnemonic outperforms a forty-item checklist, because you can run it from memory in a dark hallway.

People includes pets. Ready.gov states directly that if local officials ask you to evacuate, your pet should evacuate too, and warns that pets left behind may end up lost, injured or worse. It also notes that many public shelters and hotels do not permit pets inside, which is a planning problem to solve in advance.

Prescriptions deserve a longer horizon than most people assume. Oregon's emergency management guidance recommends a seven-day medication supply in the go-kit, against a three-day supply of water and food. Pharmacies close, records are inaccessible, and a refill in an unfamiliar town takes days you will not have.

Papers means identity documents, insurance policies, deeds, titles and a printed contact list. Photographs of every document on a phone plus a copy in cloud storage covers most cases, but a physical folder still matters when the network is saturated and the power is out.

Where the bag lives, and how often to touch it

Ready.gov's wildfire guidance says to have enough supplies for your household, including a first aid kit, in your go bag or car trunk. The trunk is the better default for anyone in a fire-prone area, because it removes the step where you have to be at home to be prepared.

Ready.gov also recommends having emergency supplies in place at home, at work and in the car. Fires do not schedule themselves around your commute, and the household that keeps one kit in a hall closet is prepared only for fires that start while everyone is home.

Rotate on a fixed date twice a yearWater, batteries, medication and infant formula all expire. Pick two dates you already remember, check the go-bag against the list above, replace anything within three months of expiry, and re-print the contact sheet. Fifteen minutes twice a year keeps the kit honest.

One more item earns its space: a printed paper map of your county with your two evacuation routes marked. Ready.gov's basic kit list includes local maps for exactly this reason. Navigation apps depend on a network that fails precisely when a large incident saturates local towers.

How do I plan routes away from wildfires near me?

Route planning is the part of preparedness that pays the largest dividend and gets the least attention, and no fire tracker near me can substitute for it. The core instruction in Ready.gov's evacuation guidance is to identify several places you could go and choose destinations in different directions so that you have options, then be familiar with alternate routes and other means of transportation out of your area.

The phrase "different directions" is the whole idea. A household with one destination has one route, and a single road closure removes their entire plan. A household with three destinations spread across three compass bearings almost always has one that is still reachable.

Building a two-route minimum

Draw your primary route first, the one you would take on an ordinary day. Then draw a second route that shares no road segment with the first for at least the first ten miles. If the two merge at a bridge, a canyon mouth or a single highway on-ramp, you effectively have one route, not two.

Ready.gov is explicit about behavior during the drive: follow recommended evacuation routes and do not take shortcuts, because they may be blocked. It also recommends planning to take one car per family to reduce congestion and delay, which is counterintuitive for households that want to save two vehicles.

The traffic argument is not theoretical. NIST's case study of the 2018 Camp Fire evacuation documents the widespread evacuation of 40,000 people complicated by high traffic volume, road closures caused by fire and debris, and direct fire exposure to civilians in numerous burnover events.

Fuel, timing and the things that strand people

Ready.gov's rule on fuel is precise and easy to follow: keep a full tank of gas if an evacuation seems likely, and keep at least a half tank at all times in case of an unexpected need to evacuate. Fuel stations close during power shutoffs and queue for an hour during orders.

Its instruction on timing is equally direct: leave early enough to avoid being trapped by severe weather. The fire equivalent is leaving at Level 2 rather than Level 3 if anything about your household slows you down, which for most people means animals, medical equipment or small children.

Fire trackers help here in a specific way. US Wildfire Tracker provides directions away from a fire through Google Maps, Apple Maps or Waze from the incident detail panel, so the bearing you are driving is chosen against the fire's actual position rather than your mental picture of it.

Practice the route before you need itReady.gov advises learning your evacuation routes and practicing with pets. Drive each route once in daylight and once after dark, note where cell coverage drops, and time it honestly. A route that takes 25 minutes empty can take 90 minutes with everyone else on it.

Finally, write the plan down. A single printed page listing three destinations, two routes, an out-of-area contact and the meeting point is more useful in a real evacuation than anything stored on a phone whose battery you will be rationing.

First 30 minutes: what to do about a fire near me

The first thirty minutes after you discover a fire near me is a sequence, not a scramble. Verify the detection, establish wind direction, check for an official order, confirm two routes, stage the vehicle and the go-bag, then set a re-check interval. Each step takes two to five minutes.

Doing them in order matters because each step changes what the next one should be. Verification prevents a wasted evacuation. Wind direction determines whether the mapped distance is shrinking or growing. An official order overrides everything else on the list, including your own reading of the map.

Minutes 0 to 10: verify and orient

Open the fire tracker near me view, set the radius to 25 miles, and identify the nearest high-probability detection. Read acres, containment, "Last NIFC update" and "Last satellite heat". A detection with a linked NIFC record and satellite heat under two hours old is real and worth planning against.

Next, establish the wind. Step outside if you safely can, or check the current conditions and any Red Flag Warning covering your county. You need two facts: which way the wind is blowing, and whether it is forecast to strengthen or shift over the next six hours.

Then check for an official evacuation order or warning covering your address. Local orders come through county alert systems, Wireless Emergency Alerts and, on many maps, an evacuation overlay. If an order exists, stop reading the map and comply with it.

Minutes 10 to 20: routes and people

Confirm both of your planned routes are open. Road closures propagate fast around an active incident and are often the first thing to change. If both routes are compromised, that is the strongest possible signal to leave immediately by whatever route remains.

Account for every person and animal, and tell them the plan in one sentence each: where you are going, which route, and where you will meet if you get separated. Ready.gov emphasizes that everyone in the household should know and understand what to do if you need to quickly evacuate.

Minutes 20 to 30: stage and set a rhythm

Put the go-bags in the vehicle, park facing out, and top up fuel if you are below half a tank. Charge phones, take the paper map, and close windows and exterior vents to slow ember and smoke entry if you end up leaving the house standing.

Set an explicit re-check interval based on distance: every 15 minutes inside 5 miles, every 30 minutes between 5 and 10, hourly out to 25. Writing the interval down prevents the two failure modes, which are refreshing obsessively and forgetting to look for two hours.

Why hardening and preparation compoundAlex Maranghides of NIST has argued that implementing current knowledge of hardening for embers, done in conjunction with addressing fire hazards, "will very significantly reduce structure losses" (U.S. Fire Administration). The work you do in a quiet week is what buys you options in a loud one.

How far can embers fly from a fire near me?

Embers, which fire scientists call firebrands, are the reason a fire tracker near me matters well outside the perimeter, and the reason a fire near me is dangerous well before the flame front arrives. NIST research indicates embers can be carried by winds and deposited several miles downwind of a wildfire, and under extreme conditions firebrands can glide up to 40 kilometers, roughly 24 miles.

That distance reframes the whole radius question. A house can sit outside a fire's mapped perimeter, outside the evacuation zone, and still receive a shower of burning material through an attic vent. This is why "the fire is 8 miles away" is a statement about the flame front, not about your risk.

Embers are the dominant ignition mechanism

The ignition statistics are stark. NIST's work on firebrands reports that embers cause up to 90 percent of home and business fires during wildfire events. Direct flame contact and radiant heat account for the remainder, which is the opposite of most people's mental model.

The U.S. Fire Administration documents one instructive example: a three-foot-wide ember, a piece of a plastic playhouse, traveled a quarter mile and ignited vegetation where it landed. Firebrands are not always sparks. They can be substantial burning objects with enough mass to survive a long flight.

Measuring ember exposure directlyNicolas Bouvet, a combustion specialist at NIST, described the goal of the agency's ember measurement work this way: "We want to be able to look at the fuel type, topography and weather, and have an idea of how serious an ember exposure might be for a structure" (NIST).

What ember reach means for reading the map

Practically, add an ember buffer to whatever distance the map shows. If a high-probability detection is 6 miles upwind under Red Flag conditions, treat your house as inside the hazard zone even though no perimeter touches it. Close windows, wet down nothing, and prepare to leave rather than to defend.

Spot fires ahead of the main front also mean the mapped perimeter systematically understates a fire's current reach. NIST's Camp Fire timeline documented spotting up to 3.9 miles into the community, with spot fires igniting in Paradise roughly 3.75 miles southwest of Concow by 8 a.m. on the first morning.

Embers also cross barriers that stop a flame front. Rivers, four-lane highways, cleared fields and fuel breaks all slow ground spread and do nothing at all against airborne firebrands. A map that shows a wide river between you and the fire is showing you less protection than it appears to.

What does defensible space do about fires near me?

Defensible space is the treated area around a structure where fuel has been reduced or removed so that an approaching fire loses intensity and firefighters have somewhere safe to work. Ready.gov's baseline instruction is to create a fire-resistant zone free of leaves, debris or flammable materials for at least 30 feet from your home.

The reason this belongs in an article about tracking fires near me is simple: the map tells you when a fire is coming, and defensible space determines what happens when it arrives. Monitoring without mitigation gives you excellent information about an outcome you cannot influence.

Start at five feet, not thirty

The U.S. Fire Administration recommends starting within Zone 1, the first five-foot area around the home, and working outward. That innermost band is where wind-driven embers accumulate against foundations, under decks and in the angle where a wall meets the ground, and it is the highest-yield work per hour spent.

Practically, the first five feet should hold nothing combustible: no bark mulch, no woodpile, no stacked recycling, no plastic furniture, no shrubs pressed against siding. The U.S. Fire Administration's ember guidance also notes the specific fix for wooden fences, using metal or stone fence material for the five feet where the fence meets the house.

Beyond five feet, the work becomes about breaking fuel continuity rather than eliminating fuel. Space shrubs apart, separate tree canopies, clear the ladder fuels that let a ground fire climb into a crown, and keep the whole zone free of the leaf litter Ready.gov names explicitly.

The scale of the exposure across the country

This is not a niche problem. Forest Service research reports that the number of houses in the wildland-urban interface grew 46 percent between 1990 and 2020, from 30 million to 44 million. The WUI covers just 9.4 percent of the land area of the continental United States but contains 32 percent of all housing.

The U.S. Fire Administration states that more than 60,000 communities in the United States are at risk for wildland-urban interface fires. Forest Service research counted more than 55,000 homes burned between 2010 and 2020, spread across far more of the country than the western-fire narrative suggests.

Where the losses actually happenMiranda Mockrin, a research scientist with the USDA Forest Service Northern Research Station, noted that news coverage "underscores a perception that wildfires are predominantly forest fires," while the team's results "highlight the prevalence of WUI losses, across the United States" (USDA Forest Service).

That finding pairs with the fuel data: grassland and shrubland fires accounted for 63.7 percent of buildings destroyed between 2000 and 2013, against 33.1 percent for forest fires. If you live beside open grass rather than timber, a fire tracker near me is still very much a tool for you.

Why does the map show fires near me that are out?

A fire tracker near me will sometimes show apparently active fires that are cold, for three structural reasons: satellite detections persist in the record after the heat is gone, official incident records are closed on agency schedules rather than in real time, and burned ground can hold enough residual heat to trigger detections for days.

Understanding this prevents both errors it causes. The first error is panic at a detection that represents yesterday's heat. The second, worse error is dismissing a live fire because you learned to distrust the map after a few stale hits.

The three timestamps that resolve it

US Wildfire Tracker exposes "Last NIFC update", "Last satellite heat" and "Last GOES heat" on the incident detail panel precisely so you can tell a live fire from a fading record. A fire whose last satellite heat is 36 hours old and whose NIFC record shows 100 percent containment is a historical artifact.

The opposite pattern is the one to act on. If "Last NIFC update" is two days old but "Last satellite heat" is 90 minutes old, the fire is doing something the paperwork has not caught up with. Trust the fresher signal about position and the official record about scale.

The app also lets you toggle outdated records on and off. Leaving them off gives a cleaner picture of what is burning now. Turning them on is useful when you are trying to understand a region's recent fire history rather than today's threat.

Containment is not extinguishment

Containment describes the percentage of a fire's edge that has a completed control line around it. A fire at 80 percent containment can still be burning vigorously in its interior and can still throw embers. Containment is a statement about the boundary, not about the fuel inside it.

Fires also get declared controlled and later declared out, and those are distinct milestones separated by days or weeks. Interior heat in heavy fuels, stumps and duff can persist through several rain events, which is why late-season satellite detections keep appearing over ground everybody stopped worrying about.

The full lifecycle of an incident record runs from a dispatch entry at ignition, through daily updates during active management, to a controlled declaration and finally an out declaration. Each of those transitions happens on an agency timetable, which is why a record can sit unchanged on the map for a day or two without meaning the fire has stopped.

Do not judge a fire tracker near me by its stale recordsEvery public wildfire map inherits the update cadence of the agencies feeding it. The right response to a stale record is to read the timestamps and the probability tier, not to stop checking. A map that occasionally shows old heat is far more useful than no map at all.

How do I check wildfires near me in a city?

Checking wildfires near me is a different exercise in a metro area than it is on a ranch. Urban and suburban readers face a different version of the problem. The fire that reaches a city rarely starts in it; it arrives from the interface at the edge, driven by wind through a corridor of grass, brush or canyon. Checking wildfires near me from a city means watching the boundary, not the downtown.

The practical consequence is that your search radius should be oriented rather than circular in your head. Identify the two or three directions where undeveloped fuel actually reaches your metro area, and treat detections in those bearings as more significant than a detection at the same distance across built-up ground.

What changes in an urban evacuation

Traffic is the dominant constraint. A neighborhood that empties onto two arterials will take far longer to clear than its mileage suggests, and Ready.gov's advice to take one car per family exists because of exactly this arithmetic. Leaving at the warning stage rather than the order stage is worth more in a city than anywhere else.

Alert coverage is usually better in metro areas: more cell sites, denser WEA coverage, more active county alert programs, and more local broadcast. But the enhanced geotargeting that WEA is capable of, delivering with no more than 528 feet of overshoot, only helps if the alerting authority draws a tight polygon.

Smoke behaves differently in a basin. Terrain that traps air overnight concentrates PM2.5 near the surface even when the fire is far away, which is why an urban AQI reading can be worse than a rural one much closer to the flame front.

How do rural readers track fires near me?

Tracking wildfires near me from rural ground trades resources for warning time. Rural households have more warning time and fewer resources. The fire may be visible for an hour before any alert is issued, the nearest engine may be 40 minutes away, and the road out may be a single gravel route that a fallen tree can close. Tracking fires near me in that setting is a different discipline.

The main advantage is line of sight. Rural readers frequently see a column before any system reports it, which makes the satellite hotspot layer disproportionately valuable: a new VIIRS or GOES detection with no linked incident record often corresponds exactly to the column on the horizon.

Connectivity is the binding constraint

Plan for the network to fail. Download or print a county map, note where coverage drops on each route, and keep a battery or hand-crank NOAA Weather Radio, which Ready.gov describes as broadcasting official warnings, watches and forecasts 24 hours a day, seven days a week from the nearest National Weather Service office.

US Wildfire Tracker refreshes automatically every few minutes while open, and if a refresh fails it displays the last saved results rather than an empty map. On a marginal rural connection that behavior matters, because a blank screen and a screen showing 40-minute-old data are very different things.

Livestock and equipment turn Level 2 into your real deadline. Loading animals takes time nobody has at Level 3, which is why Oregon's guidance explicitly names livestock owners among those who should be ready to move at Level 1 and should evacuate voluntarily at Level 2.

Agree on a rally point outside the valleyRural households often lose contact with each other before they lose the road. Pick a fixed meeting place well outside your immediate area, tell everyone including seasonal workers, and name a single out-of-area contact everyone calls. It removes the worst decision from the worst moment.

How does smoke change what a fire near me means?

Smoke changes the question from "should I leave" to "should I be breathing this." Those are separate decisions with separate thresholds, and a fire tracker near me is only useful if you keep them separate. Air quality can be dangerous at 300 miles and fine at 15.

The Air Quality Index is the tool for the second decision. EPA's AirNow describes six color-coded categories, and notes that an AQI value of 100 generally corresponds to the level of the short-term national ambient air quality standard for protection of public health.

Why the particle size drives the health risk

EPA defines PM2.5 as fine inhalable particles with diameters generally 2.5 micrometers and smaller. For scale, the agency notes the average human hair is about 70 micrometers in diameter, making it roughly 30 times larger than the largest fine particle. Particles that small bypass the body's normal filtration.

That is why Ready.gov's wildfire guidance names the N95 mask twice: store one to protect yourself from smoke inhalation, and use one during a wildfire. It is also why the guidance says that if you are not ordered to evacuate but smoky conditions exist, stay inside in a safe location.

The research is still catching up to the exposureMonika Kopacz, Ph.D., a NOAA program manager for atmospheric chemistry, carbon cycle and climate, put the gap this way: "Measurements show us a snapshot, but models help us understand how chemicals move and change in the atmosphere. Until we unravel the full picture, millions of people remain at risk, breathing in dangerous smoke we still don't fully understand" (NOAA Chemical Sciences Laboratory).

Satellites nowcast smoke the way they detect fire

The same geostationary platforms that provide GOES heat detections also track smoke. NOAA's satellites orbit 22,236 miles above the equator and their imagers can produce images as frequently as every 30 seconds, which is what makes short-horizon smoke nowcasting possible at all.

Shobha Kondragunta, Ph.D., who leads air quality studies at NOAA's Center for Satellite Applications and Research, said that NOAA's geostationary satellite data of fine particulate pollution "increasingly are being used in nowcasting mode to provide warnings to the public." The lead time that creates is the practical benefit for households.

What the alert vocabulary actually means

The National Weather Service issues a Red Flag Warning, in conjunction with land management agencies, to alert land managers to an ongoing or imminent critical fire weather pattern. A Fire Weather Watch means critical fire weather conditions are possible but not imminent or occurring.

Ready.gov's toolkit puts numbers on the timing: a Fire Weather Watch is issued when potentially dangerous fire weather conditions are possible over the next 12 to 72 hours, while a Red Flag Warning is issued when conditions occur or are expected to occur within 24 hours.

The NWS also defines extreme fire behavior, the phrase you will see in incident updates, by four indicators: a high rate of spread, prolific crowning or spotting, the presence of fire whirls, and a strong convection column. When those words appear in an update about a fire near you, the mapped perimeter is already out of date.

An alert about weather is not an alert about a fireA Red Flag Warning covering your county with no detections inside 50 miles means the conditions are dangerous, not that anything is burning. Conversely, a fire five miles upwind with no Red Flag Warning is still a fire. Read the two layers separately, then combine them.

Is a fire tracker near me the same as an evacuation order?

No. A fire tracker near me is an informational map built from public agency feeds. It does not issue orders, dispatch resources or make legal determinations. Evacuation orders come from your county emergency management office or sheriff, delivered through Wireless Emergency Alerts and county alert systems. When an order conflicts with anything on a map, follow the order without hesitation.

Why can I smell smoke when no fire near me appears?

Smoke routinely travels hundreds or thousands of miles from its source. In June 2023, NOAA reported more than 86 million people breathing fine particulate pollution above the federal health standard from Canadian fires, including more than 17 million in Very Unhealthy air in three East Coast cities. Empty map plus heavy haze usually means transported smoke, not a local ignition.

NASA states that VIIRS 375 m active fire data are available globally within three hours of satellite observation, and in real time for the United States and Canada. Why does a fire near me show as active when it is out?

Three causes. Burned ground holds residual heat for days and keeps triggering detections. Official incident records are closed on agency schedules rather than immediately. And satellite detections persist in the historical record. Check "Last satellite heat" against "Last NIFC update": if both are days old and containment reads 100 percent, the record is historical.

Does a Red Flag Warning mean there is a fire near me?

No. A Red Flag Warning describes weather, not fire. The National Weather Service glossary sets the criteria as sustained winds averaging 15 mph or greater, relative humidity at or below 25 percent, and temperature above 75 degrees Fahrenheit with fire danger already high to extreme. It means any ignition would spread dangerously, wherever that ignition occurs.

What should be in a go-bag for a wildfire?

Ready.gov's baseline is one gallon of water per person per day for several days, several days of non-perishable food, a first aid kit, flashlight, batteries, a NOAA Weather Radio, a whistle, local paper maps and a charged phone with a backup battery. For wildfire specifically, add N95 masks. Remember the Five Ps: People, Prescriptions, Papers, Personal Needs, Priceless Items.

Do I need an app, or is the website enough?

The website at wildfire-map.org is a complete free live map in any browser and requires no account. The Chrome extension exists to remove friction: it opens the same map in a side panel or full tab beside whatever you are already doing, which matters when the useful behavior is a short daily check rather than an emergency search.

How often does a fire tracker near me refresh?

US Wildfire Tracker refreshes automatically every few minutes while the map is open, and there is a manual refresh button. If a refresh attempt fails, it displays the last saved results rather than an empty map, which matters on marginal connections. The underlying agency feeds still update on their own schedules, so read the timestamps.

How much wildfire activity is normal in a US year?

Averaging the ten years 2016 through 2025 in NIFC's national table gives roughly 63,200 wildland fires and about 7.05 million acres per year. The range is wide: 2020 burned 10,122,336 acres while 2023 burned 2,693,910. Fire counts and acreage move independently, so a high-count year is not automatically a high-acreage year.
