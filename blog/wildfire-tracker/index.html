What does a wildfire tracker actually show you?

A wildfire tracker layers four independent data streams on one map: official incident records from federal and state agencies, mapped fire perimeters, satellite thermal detections, and fire-weather alerts issued by the National Weather Service. US Wildfire Tracker combines all four and adds a probability score to each detection.

Those four streams answer four different questions. The incident record tells you a fire exists and has a name, an acreage and a containment figure. The perimeter tells you where its edge was when it was last mapped. The satellite detection tells you where heat was radiating during the last overpass. The alert tells you whether the weather is about to make everything worse.

Confusing them is the single most common mistake in wildfire tracking, and it is the reason a wildfire tracker has to label its layers rather than blend them. A satellite hotspot is not a fire. It is a pixel in which an instrument measured a mid-infrared signal strong enough to trigger a detection algorithm. NOAA notes that those algorithms begin to respond when an active fire occupies about 0.01 percent of the pixel footprint at a temperature of roughly 800 K or above.

The incident record: the fire's identity document

An incident record is the authoritative entry an agency creates when a fire is reported. It carries the fire name, discovery date, acreage, containment percentage, incident type, managing organization and, when the agency supplies it, total assigned personnel. On US Wildfire Tracker every one of those fields appears in the per-fire detail panel alongside a "Last NIFC update" timestamp.

That timestamp matters more than the acreage. Agencies refresh incident records on their own schedules, usually after a morning or evening operational briefing. A record showing 40 percent containment may be describing a situation that changed twelve hours ago. Reading the timestamp before the number is the habit that separates useful wildfire tracking from false confidence, and every good wildfire tracker puts that timestamp beside the figure.

The perimeter: the shape, not the edge

A perimeter is a polygon drawn from infrared aircraft flights, GPS tracks walked or driven by crews, or satellite imagery. It represents the fire's mapped boundary at a specific moment. Perimeters typically update once or twice a day on active large incidents and far less often on small ones, so a fresh-looking outline can be a day old.

US Wildfire Tracker draws the official perimeter for incidents that have one, and can also draw a historical perimeter so you can see how the footprint grew. Where no perimeter exists, the wildfire tracker falls back to a point marker, which is normal for new starts and for the thousands of small fires that never get flown.

The satellite hotspot: heat, with a clock on it

Thermal detections come from three families of instrument. MODIS flies on NASA's Terra and Aqua satellites, launched in December 1999 and May 2002, and detects hot spots at 1,000 meters per pixel. VIIRS flies on Suomi NPP, launched October 2011, and NOAA-20, launched November 2017, and resolves 375 meters per pixel. GOES sits in geostationary orbit and scans continuously.

NASA's Earth Observatory puts the difference plainly: VIIRS makes fire observations about three times more detailed than MODIS, so it detects many small and low-temperature fires that MODIS misses. That is why a modern wildfire tracker, and any serious wild fire tracker built on the same feeds, weights a recent VIIRS detection more heavily than an older MODIS one when it scores confidence.

Why higher resolution changed field practiceWilfrid Schroeder, a scientist at the University of Maryland and principal investigator for the VIIRS active fire data product, said the goal is practical: "We hope that by infusing the higher-resolution fire detection data and fire behavior modeling outputs into tactical fire situations, we can lessen the pressure on those working in wildfire management." Read the full explanation at NASA Earth Observatory.

The fire-weather alert: tomorrow's problem, today

National Weather Service Red Flag Warnings and Fire Weather Watches are the only layer on a wildfire tracker or wild fire tracker that looks forward rather than backward. The NWS glossary defines Red Flag criteria as a dry spell plus a National Fire Danger Rating System reading of high to extreme, with sustained wind averaging 15 mph or more, relative humidity at or below 25 percent, and temperature above 75 degrees Fahrenheit.

Local offices tighten or loosen those thresholds for their own fuels. The NWS Chicago office, for example, uses sustained 20-foot winds of 20 mph or higher, afternoon relative humidity under 25 percent, and 10-hour fuel moisture at 8 percent or less. A Fire Weather Watch goes out up to 72 hours ahead; the Red Flag Warning follows within 24 hours of the event.

The probability score: a way to rank the noise

On a busy August afternoon a national hotspot feed behind a wildfire tracker can contain thousands of points, and only a fraction of them are wildfires. US Wildfire Tracker assigns each detection a probability tier that fuses three things: whether an official NIFC record is linked to it, how fresh the NIFC perimeter is, and how recent the VIIRS, MODIS or GOES heat signal is.

Detections scoring below 25 percent are hidden by default as weak signals and can be switched on when you want the full picture. Outdated records can also be toggled back on. The app warns explicitly that a hotspot may reflect a prescribed burn or an industrial heat source rather than a wildfire, which is exactly the caution NOAA attaches to its own data.

What a probability tier is notThe score ranks how likely a detection is to represent a real, currently burning wildfire. It is not a threat level, a spread forecast or an evacuation trigger. Evacuation decisions come from your county sheriff, fire district or emergency management office, and nothing on any map replaces them.

If you want the mechanics of each layer in more depth, the companion guide on how a fire tracker works end to end walks through the same stack from ignition to containment, including the detection algorithms themselves.

Which agencies stand behind wild fire tracking?

Wild fire tracking in the United States rests on ten Geographic Area Coordination Centers, each responsible for mobilizing crews, aircraft and equipment inside its own region and for publishing the intelligence products a wildfire tracker consumes. Understanding those ten centers is the fastest route to competent wildfire tracking anywhere in the country. The National Interagency Fire Center in Boise, Idaho, coordinates between them.

The ten centers are Alaska, Eastern Area, Great Basin, Northern California, Northern Rockies, Northwest, Rocky Mountain, Southern Area, Southern California and Southwest. They are not identical. The Southern Area alone covers 13 states plus Puerto Rico and the US Virgin Islands, stretching from Virginia south to the Gulf and west to Texas.

Each center posts a preparedness level from 1 to 5 that summarizes how stretched its resources are. On September 10, 2026 the Rocky Mountain Area sat at level 2 against a national level of 3, the Northwest sat at 3, and Alaska sat at 1 as its season closed. Those numbers are the fastest single read on where the national firefighting system is under strain.

Who owns the ground changes who owns the data

Land ownership decides which agency writes the incident record. The US Forest Service manages national forests, the Bureau of Land Management manages public rangeland, the National Park Service manages parks, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs and Fish and Wildlife Service manage their own estates. State forestry agencies protect state and most private land.

The Northwest Coordination Center coordinates across all of them for Oregon and Washington: BLM, the Forest Service, the Oregon Department of Forestry, Fish and Wildlife, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, the Washington Department of Natural Resources and the Park Service. A single fire crossing an ownership boundary can therefore appear in two agencies' feeds with two different acreage figures on the same afternoon.

That is precisely the problem a wildfire tracker exists to solve, and it is why a single-agency page is not a substitute for one. Rather than asking you to check eight agency pages, a wildfire tracker pulls interagency records and perimeters from the NIFC and WFIGS services, satellite heat from NASA and NOAA, and alerts from the National Weather Service API, then presents one incident with one detail panel and official links back to the source.

Read the managing organization fieldThe per-fire panel names the organization in charge. If it reads a national forest, expect InciWeb updates and infrared flights. If it reads a state forestry agency, expect faster small-fire reporting but fewer mapped perimeters. If it reads a county or rural fire district, expect the record to close quickly once the fire is out.

Human causes dominate, but not everywhere

NIFC publishes ignition causes broken out by geographic area, and the regional contrast is stark. In 2025 the Southern Area logged 36,040 human-caused wildfires and only 1,048 lightning fires. The Northwest logged 2,936 human-caused starts against 1,682 from lightning. Alaska recorded 248 lightning fires, more than its 217 human-caused ones.

Nationally, NIFC's published totals for 2025 were 69,556 human-caused wildfires and 8,294 lightning-caused wildfires, adding to the 77,850 fires the agency recorded for the year, according to the agency's human-caused and lightning-caused statistics tables.

The practical consequence for wild fire tracking is timing, and it should change how often you refresh a wildfire tracker. Where humans start most fires, ignitions cluster around holidays, weekends, road corridors and property boundaries, and they happen at all hours. Where lightning dominates, ignitions arrive in storm-driven bursts and often smoulder for days before a satellite sees them.

Pacific Northwest: what does a wildfire tracker show?

In Oregon and Washington a wildfire tracker spends most of July, August and September east of the Cascade crest, and wildfire tracking west of the crest is largely a smoke exercise. The Northwest Coordination Center in Portland runs the region, and its 2026 season was severe enough that Washington reported 18 large uncontained fires burning more than 450,000 acres with over 8,000 personnel assigned.

The Cascades split the region into two fire climates. West of the crest, Douglas-fir and hemlock forests are wet enough that fires are rare but capable of extreme behavior when an east wind arrives. East of the crest, ponderosa pine, sagebrush steppe and the Columbia Basin's grass and wheat stubble cure early and burn fast.

The east wind is the variable that matters

NIFC's September 1, 2026 outlook described how a single dry cold front on August 1 produced sustained 20 to 30 mph winds with gusts of 40 to 50 mph across eastern Washington, the Columbia Basin and eastern Oregon. Three new large fires near Spokane damaged or destroyed more than 800 structures inside the city.

Predictive Services put the probability of at least one moderately strong east-wind day in September at elevated, noting that three of the four La Nina to El Nino transitions since 1979 produced a day with a 90th-percentile east-wind pressure gradient. When your wildfire tracker shows a Red Flag Warning for an east-wind event in this region, treat it as a category change, not a nuance. No other single input shifts Pacific Northwest wild fire tracking as sharply.

Two state officials on dry fuels plus windRuss Lane, Wildland Fire Division Manager at the Washington Department of Natural Resources, said during an August 2026 warning: "The combination of extremely dry fuels, windy conditions and the number of incidents on the landscape already could make this a dynamic situation for any new ignitions in the coming days." In the same release, Commissioner of Public Lands Dave Upthegrove said: "Thousands of firefighters are working day and night to control one of the most destructive fire seasons in state history." Both statements are on the Washington DNR news page.

Who protects what, and why acreage figures differ

The Oregon Department of Forestry describes itself as the state's largest fire department and protects 16 million acres of forest, an asset it values at $60 billion. Oregon does not run one statewide fire season: the State Forester declares fire season district by district, so southern Oregon is typically under restriction weeks before northwest Oregon.

Washington DNR runs its own protection districts and imposed a burn ban across its Northeast and Southeast Regions from July 17, 2026. Because state, federal and private protection interleave, the same fire can be reported by two organizations. A wildfire tracker that keys on the interagency record avoids showing you the same incident twice, which is one practical test of whether a wild fire tracker is fusing data or merely stacking it.

California: how do you track wild fires year-round?

California is the only state a wildfire tracker must watch in every month, which makes year-round wildfire tracking a requirement rather than a preference. Two coordination centers split it: Northern California operations in Redding and Southern California operations in Riverside. Northern California typically records eight significant fires in September, four in October and one or fewer in November and December.

That statistical curve is the backbone of California wild fire tracking. The north burns on a summer timber-and-lightning cycle. The south burns on an autumn and early-winter wind cycle that runs long after the north has cooled. A wildfire tracker configured for one half of the state will miss the other half's peak entirely.

Northern California: lightning sieges and dry fuels

Predictive Services in Redding calculates regional Energy Release Component values from 122 Remote Automated Weather Stations. In August 2026 those values sat near or above the 85th percentile for most of the month and touched near-record highs from August 3 to 11 against a 21-year dataset.

Lightning is the wildcard. The 2000 to 2025 Vaisala August average for northern California is nearly 8,400 strikes; through August 26, 2026 fewer than 300 had been observed. A quiet lightning month keeps ignition counts down, but it does nothing to rehydrate fuels, so the risk simply waits for the next ignition source.

Ignition volume tells the same story from the other side. Northern California averaged 15.1 daily wildfire ignitions through August 26, 2026, below the 21.2 daily average for August across 2008 to 2025. Six large fires were reported through August 26 against a regional August significant-fire average of 16.8 across 1992 to 2025.

Southern California: the offshore wind calendar

Southern California's fire year is governed by offshore winds. Through most of August 2026 the region ran 2 to 8 degrees Fahrenheit above normal with routine afternoon winds of 15 to 25 mph gusting to 40 mph over mountain ridges and through desert passes, and live fuel moisture mostly between 60 and 80 percent.

Live fuel moisture is the number that decides whether an offshore wind event produces a nuisance fire or a disaster. When chaparral drops toward the low 60s and an offshore gradient sets up, a wildfire tracker's Red Flag polygon and its nearest hotspot cluster become the two things worth refreshing every few minutes. That pairing is the core of southern California wild fire tracking.

Predictive Services expected fewer Santa Ana events than normal in autumn 2026 and thought the first Gulf of Alaska storm would likely arrive before the first Santa Ana wind event. That sequence matters: a wet frontal system ahead of the wind season shortens the dangerous window considerably. For county-level detail, see the dedicated California fire map guide.

Red Flag counts are not a season forecastThe National Weather Service issued Red Flag Warnings on 10 separate days in northern California in August 2026, almost exactly the 10.1-day August average for 2006 to 2025. An average warning count in a drought year still produces above-normal fire potential, because the warnings measure weather and the fuels are a separate variable.

Southwest: when does wild fire tracking matter most?

The Southwest inverts the national calendar. Arizona and New Mexico burn hardest from April into early July, before the North American Monsoon arrives, and a wild fire tracking routine built for August peaks will miss the region's most dangerous six weeks entirely. Set your wildfire tracker for spring here, not late summer.

The Southwest Coordination Center manages both states through dispatch centers in Albuquerque, Alamogordo, Silver City, Prescott, Springerville and Tucson. Fires here run through ponderosa pine at elevation, pinyon-juniper at mid-slope, and grass and chaparral below, and the elevation band a fire occupies predicts its behavior more reliably than its acreage does.

The monsoon is the switch, and it is unreliable

Monsoon onset normally ends the Southwest's critical period. In 2026 it did not arrive cleanly. NIFC reported the August monsoon was weaker than normal because of disruptions from Pacific tropical systems, with eastern New Mexico running 5 to 10 degrees Fahrenheit above normal and most locations recording less than an inch of rain for most of the month.

NIFC also noted large fires emerging in New Mexico in early August, which the agency called very unusual. That is the signal to watch on a wildfire tracker in this region, and the one most casual wild fire tracker users overlook: not a single big fire, but new large-fire starts appearing in a month that should be wet. Many storms produced lightning while remaining too dry or short-lived to help.

Track dry lightning separately from wet lightningIn the Southwest a thunderstorm can deliver hundreds of strikes and no useful rainfall. Watch for clusters of new satellite hotspots appearing 24 to 72 hours after a storm passes. Holdover fires smoulder in duff and roots, then surface when relative humidity drops, which is why hotspot counts often rise days after the storm.

What a Southwest incident record looks like

Roughly a third of Arizona and New Mexico is federal land, so most significant incidents carry a Forest Service, Park Service, BLM or Bureau of Indian Affairs managing organization. Those incidents get InciWeb pages, mapped perimeters and infrared flights, which makes the Southwest one of the better-documented regions on any wildfire tracker or forest fire tracker.

NIFC's 2025 cause statistics show 1,658 human-caused fires and 821 lightning-caused fires in the Southwest geographic area. That near two-to-one ratio understates lightning's importance, because lightning fires here start in remote high country and are far more likely to grow large before anyone reaches them.

Rockies: what does a wildfire tracker show at altitude?

The Rockies split across three coordination centers: Northern Rockies for Montana, northern Idaho and the Dakotas; Rocky Mountain, based in Lakewood, Colorado, for Colorado, Wyoming, Nebraska, Kansas and South Dakota; and Great Basin, based in Salt Lake City, for Nevada, Utah and southern Idaho. A wildfire tracker set to a single state here will miss the regional picture, so treat wildfire tracking in the Rockies as a three-center job.

Elevation controls everything. Fires that start in valley grass move fast and die at the timber edge. Fires that establish in subalpine fir and lodgepole above 8,000 feet can burn for weeks, and their perimeters change slowly enough that a daily map refresh is genuinely sufficient.

Terrain breaks satellite geolocation

NOAA warns that fire pixels detected over mountain ranges and steep terrain can show larger locational errors introduced by the terrain correction applied to satellite geolocation data. In practice a hotspot in the Bitterroots or the San Juans can plot several hundred meters from the actual flame front.

That is why a wildfire tracker's probability score should weight a linked official record above a raw detection in mountainous country. Mountain wild fire tracking is the clearest case for scoring rather than plotting. If a cluster of hotspots sits near a named incident with a mapped perimeter, trust the perimeter for location and the hotspots for activity. If the hotspots stand alone on a ridge, treat the position as approximate.

The Great Basin is the region's fast-moving half

Cheatgrass and sagebrush respond to weather in hours rather than days. NIFC recorded the Great Basin moving to preparedness level 5 at the start of August 2026, dropping back to 3 after mid-month moisture, then returning to 4 as activity surged again. Few regions swing that hard inside four weeks.

The agency also flagged a pattern worth building into your habits: areas that received recent wetting rains saw fire activity resume and increase quickly after only a few warm, dry days. In cheatgrass country a wildfire tracker showing a quiet week means very little, and complacent wild fire tracking is genuinely risky. Fine fuels can return to critical dryness in 48 to 72 hours.

Live fuel moisture in low-elevation Great Basin brush sat mostly between 60 and 90 percent entering September 2026, which NIFC described as supporting extreme fire behavior. Watch for a secondary grass crop after late-season rain, which emerged in eastern Utah and the Arizona Strip and adds a fresh fine-fuel layer to burn later.

Colorado, Wyoming and the wind window

The Rocky Mountain Area returned to normal significant fire potential for September through December 2026, but NIFC warned that autumn brings stronger wind events and that brief periods of elevated potential may occur after stretches of warm, dry weather, generally lasting no more than one or two days.

Those one- and two-day windows are exactly what a fire-weather alert layer is for. In August 2026 most of the Rocky Mountain Area received 75 percent of average precipitation or more, while southwest Wyoming and northwest Colorado were below 25 percent. A wildfire tracker that shows drought-driven contrast within a single state is doing its job, and that contrast is what makes state-level wildfire tracking worth the effort here.

Great Plains: why is wild fire tracking about wind?

On the Great Plains a wildfire tracker is really a wind tracker, and wild fire tracking here is a matter of minutes rather than days. Grass fires here can run at highway speed across open country, and the interval between a satellite detection and a fire reaching a ranch road is often shorter than the satellite's own processing latency. Texas alone recorded 1,078 wildfires for 301,528 acres through early September 2026.

Texas A&M Forest Service is the lead agency for wildfire response in Texas and listed a state preparedness level of 3 in September 2026. Oklahoma Forestry Services, the Kansas Forest Service and Nebraska's state and county systems play the same role further north, working alongside thousands of volunteer fire departments.

Flash drought is the Plains-specific hazard

August 2026 produced a textbook example. Texas A&M Forest Service responded to 313 wildfires for 121,774 acres in August alone, and NIFC forecast above normal significant fire potential across Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi and southwestern Alabama for September.

A state fire analyst on flash droughtWalter Flocke, fire analyst at Texas A&M Forest Service, said: "The rapid onset of a flash drought increased fire occurrence and intensity statewide in August. Under flash drought conditions, grasses, brush and timber fuels lose moisture rapidly and become susceptible to ignition." His colleague Jared Karns, the agency's fire chief, added: "Even a small spark can quickly ignite a wildfire under the right conditions." Both statements are in the agency's September 2, 2026 news release.

Flash drought matters for wild fire tracking because it decouples fire risk from the seasonal calendar. Fuels that were green in July can be receptive in mid-August. The wildfire tracker layer that reflects this fastest is the fire-weather alert, because NWS offices adjust Red Flag criteria to the fuel state they are actually observing.

Human ignition dominates, and prevention is measurable

Texas A&M Forest Service states that nine out of 10 wildfires in Texas are human-caused. Debris burning is the leading human cause and equipment use, including vehicles, trailers and ATVs, is second. Parking a hot vehicle on cured grass is a documented ignition mechanism, not folklore.

Burn bans on the Plains are hyper-local. Each Texas county sets and lifts its own burn ban through the county judge or commissioners court, which means a 60-mile drive can cross three different legal regimes in an afternoon. A statewide map will not tell you the rule where you are standing.

Southeast: how does a forest fire tracker read smoke?

The Southeast burns more often than anywhere else in the country and makes headlines least. NIFC recorded 36,040 human-caused wildfires in the Southern Area in 2025, more than every western coordination center combined. Most are small, fast and extinguished within hours, which makes a forest fire tracker's probability tiers essential rather than optional. Nowhere else does wildfire tracking depend so heavily on filtering.

The Southern Area covers 13 states from Virginia to Texas plus Puerto Rico and the US Virgin Islands. Its fire season is bimodal: a late-winter and early-spring peak from February to April before green-up, and an autumn peak after leaf drop, with drought-driven exceptions in any month.

Prescribed fire is the region's defining signal

The South burns more acres on purpose than the rest of the country burns by accident. NIFC recorded 6,058,508 acres of prescribed fire nationally in 2019, of which 4,236,882 acres were burned by state and other non-federal partners, the great majority of that in southern pine.

For anyone using a forest fire tracker in Georgia, Florida, Alabama or the Carolinas, this is the number one cause of confusion. A field of satellite hotspots in longleaf pine in February, seen on any wildfire tracker, is far more likely to be a planned burn than a wildfire. That is why US Wildfire Tracker states plainly that a detection may reflect a prescribed burn, and why the probability tier that checks for a linked NIFC incident record matters so much here.

Do not report a prescribed burn as a wildfirePrescribed burns are conducted under a written burn plan with weather prescriptions and notification to local fire departments. If you see a hotspot with no linked incident record, in a managed pine stand, on a mild winter afternoon, check your state forestry agency's burn authorization system before calling it in.

Storm debris changes the fuel model for years

NIFC's 2026 Southern Area assessment listed fuel concerns ranging from recently cured fine fuels to drought and beetle kill, along with tornado, ice storm and hurricane damage. It singled out forests across the southern Appalachians, Piedmont and coastal plain damaged by Hurricane Helene, noting that heavy fuels in the hurricane's footprint consume completely once ignited.

That is a multi-year condition. Downed timber does not disappear after one season, so a wildfire tracker user in western North Carolina or upstate South Carolina should expect longer-duration incidents in storm-damaged terrain than the region's historic averages suggest. Our South Carolina fires guide covers that state's tracking specifics in detail.

South Florida is the region's other outlier. NIFC described late-summer 2026 drought there as among the most intense on record for the season, with extreme drought coverage second only to 2015, and forecast above normal significant fire potential for the peninsula in September. Low water levels in the Everglades are themselves a fire risk factor.

Alaska: what makes wild fire tracking different there?

Alaska is the one place in the United States where lightning starts more fires than people do. NIFC recorded 248 lightning-caused fires against 217 human-caused fires in Alaska in 2025. Wildfire tracking here works on a compressed calendar, in terrain with almost no roads, and against fuels that behave unlike anything in the lower 48.

The Alaska Interagency Coordination Center sits on Fort Wainwright near Fairbanks and coordinates the Bureau of Land Management, the State of Alaska Department of Natural Resources, the Forest Service, the Park Service, the Bureau of Indian Affairs and the Fish and Wildlife Service. Wildland fires are reported statewide to 1-800-237-3633.

Black spruce and duff burn on a different clock

Alaska's Interior burns in black spruce, shrub and grass over deep organic duff. Duff layers hold heat for weeks, so a fire declared quiet can resurface after rain. That single property changes how you read a wildfire tracker in Alaska: a low containment percentage on an Alaskan fire is normal, not alarming, and often reflects strategy rather than loss of control.

The 2026 Mukluk Fire near Tok illustrates the pattern. It started August 17, 2026 in black spruce, shrubs and grass, reached 7,335 acres at 29 percent containment, and destroyed 50 single residences plus 108 outbuildings, garages, sheds and workshops. NIFC described it as emerging during a very dry and windy day and immediately requiring a Complex Incident Management Team.

Read Alaskan containment differentlyAlaska manages fire by protection option. Many remote fires are monitored rather than suppressed because there is nothing at risk and no way to get crews there. A fire at 0 percent containment inside a Limited protection area is being managed as intended. Check the managing organization and the fire's distance from communities before drawing conclusions.

The season starts and ends abruptly

Alaska's fire season runs roughly May through early August, driven by long daylight and Interior thunderstorms. It ends when the rain arrives. NIFC's September 2026 outlook recorded season-ending rains beginning in the last week of August, dropping fire weather indices to low across the western two-thirds of the state.

Snow normally reaches most Interior locations by mid-October and pushes south of the Alaska Range into south-central Alaska and the Copper River Basin by early November, effectively ending the fire year. Alaska's preparedness level had already fallen to 1 by September 9, 2026, the lowest on the five-point scale.

Because the season is short and intense, Alaskan users should treat a wildfire tracker as a June-and-July instrument, and treat wild fire tracking as effectively closed by late September. Configure wildfire tracker proximity alerts before breakup and expect the map to go quiet in September. Smoke, however, outlasts flame: Interior smoke can reduce air quality in Fairbanks and Anchorage long after fire growth stops.

Hawaii: why do the islands need a wildfire tracker?

Hawaii needs a wildfire tracker as much as any mainland state: it burns a larger share of its land area in a typical year than most western states, and almost every fire is started by a person. University of Hawaii research found that over 99 percent of known wildfire causes in Hawaii are due to human activities, unlike much of the mainland where lightning contributes heavily.

The scale surprises people. Research summarized by the university found the state averaging more than 1,000 fires burning over 20,000 acres each year in the 2005 to 2011 study period, with the area burned annually having increased more than fourfold in recent decades.

A wildfire researcher on island fireClay Trauernicht, wildfire extension specialist at the University of Hawaii at Manoa, said: "People don't typically think of wildfire as a frequent disturbance on tropical islands." He has attributed the increase to non-native grasses such as guinea grass and haole koa, and reports that annual area burned has risen by 300 percent across all four counties of Hawaii in recent decades. The research summary is published by the University of Hawaii.

Abandoned agriculture created the fuel bed

The underlying cause is land-use change. Land under active grazing or cultivation in Hawaii fell from about 2.6 million acres in 1960 to roughly 970,000 acres, a reduction of more than 60 percent. Dry non-native grasslands and shrublands now compose 24 percent of Hawaii's total land cover.

Those grasslands sit between neighborhoods and forest, which is why ignitions cluster in developed areas while the largest burned areas occur in grass. The published research found that 95 percent of Hawaii fires were under 4 hectares, while 93 percent of the area burned came from fires of 40 hectares or more.

Hawaii's Red Flag criteria are their own thing

Do not apply mainland thresholds here. The NWS Honolulu criteria updated for 2026 trigger a Red Flag event on leeward portions of the state when relative humidity is at or below 45 percent for two or more hours and sustained wind is at or above 26 mph for two or more hours at specific stations, with Keetch-Byram Drought Index eligibility.

Warnings are issued island by island, which is the right granularity for a place where windward and leeward conditions differ by a factor of ten in rainfall. When you filter a wildfire tracker to Hawaii, the alert polygon your wild fire tracking depends on is the one covering your island's leeward side, published by the NWS Honolulu forecast office.

Hawaii's 2026 late summer was quiet by these measures. NIFC recorded no Red Flag Warnings in Hawaii during August 2026, with the largest fire of the month burning a little over 200 acres on the Big Island on August 11. Hurricane Lala passed August 14 to 16 and dropped up to 40 inches on parts of the island, though NIFC noted its strong winds increased hazard fuel loading for later.

Homeowners: how to set a wildfire tracker for a house?

A homeowner needs a wildfire tracker to answer one question reliably, and everything else in wildfire tracking is secondary to it: is anything burning that could reach my address, and how much time would I have. Everything else is secondary. That means configuring for proximity and bearing rather than for national coverage.

Start by enabling browser geolocation so the wildfire tracker can list fires near you within a set distance in miles. US Wildfire Tracker uses location only to find nearby fires and does not store, sell or share it. Then choose a working radius: 5 miles for immediate threat, 25 miles for planning, 50 miles for smoke.

  1. Open the live map at wildfire-map.org, allow location, and confirm your position on the basemap.
  2. Set the nearby distance so the fire list panel shows incidents within 25 miles of your address.
  3. Turn on the fire-weather alert layer and check whether a Red Flag Warning or Fire Weather Watch covers your county today.
  4. For every fire in the list, open the detail panel and read three fields in order: last NIFC update, last satellite heat, and percent contained.
  5. Note the bearing from the fire to your house, then compare it with the forecast wind direction for the next 12 hours.
  6. If a fire is within 10 miles and upwind, check your county emergency management channel for evacuation status before doing anything else.

Why bearing beats distance

Distance alone is a weak predictor. A fire 15 miles upwind on a Red Flag day is a more serious problem than a fire 4 miles downwind with a mapped perimeter that has not grown in 36 hours. Reading the wind direction alongside the fire's position converts a static wildfire tracker into a rough time estimate.

The Great Plains wind-shift problem applies to houses everywhere. If a front is forecast to swing the wind 90 degrees, the fire's long flank becomes a broad head fire. Anyone who lives along that flank moves from safe to threatened in the time it takes the front to pass.

A tracker is not an emergency serviceUS Wildfire Tracker is an informational map. It is not an official emergency service and it does not replace evacuation orders. Satellite detections can lag by minutes to hours and can miss small or smouldering fires under cloud, and official perimeters update on agency schedules. Always follow the instructions of local authorities.

Set up before the season, not during it

The worst time to learn a wildfire tracker's controls is during an evacuation warning. Do the wildfire tracker configuration in the quiet season: install the extension, grant location, set your radius, learn where the refresh button is, and practise reading a detail panel on a fire that does not concern you.

Also decide in advance what you will do at each trigger. Many western counties use a three-stage system commonly described as Ready, Set, Go, with the last stage meaning leave immediately. Deciding which stage prompts you to load the car is a decision to make in June, not at 2 a.m. in October.

Hikers: what wild fire tracking do you do pre-trip?

Hikers and backpackers face a specific version of the problem: you need to make one good decision before you lose connectivity, then live with it for days. Wild fire tracking for a trip is therefore a planning exercise, not a monitoring exercise, and a wildfire tracker should be part of the packing routine.

Do the check the evening before departure and again at the trailhead. Look for active incidents anywhere inside your route corridor plus a generous buffer, any Red Flag Warning covering the days you will be out, and any fire restrictions on the managing agency's own page linked from the incident record.

  1. Filter the wildfire tracker to the state you will be hiking in and scan the full fire list, not just the nearest few.
  2. Identify every incident within 30 miles of your route, including small ones with no mapped perimeter.
  3. Check the fire-weather layer for Red Flag Warnings or Fire Weather Watches covering your trip dates.
  4. Open the official links in each incident record to check trail, road and area closures before you drive.
  5. Screenshot the wildfire tracker and the fire list so you carry the last known picture into the backcountry.
  6. Agree a bail-out trigger and an alternate exit trailhead with everyone in the party.

Smoke will affect you before flame does

Statistically the far more likely wildfire impact on a hike is smoke, not entrapment. Smoke travels hundreds of miles and can degrade air quality in valleys with no fire within a day's drive. The EPA Air Quality Index uses six color-coded categories, and an AQI value of 100 generally corresponds to the level of the short-term national ambient air quality standard.

Above 150, described as Unhealthy, some members of the general public may experience health effects and sensitive groups more serious ones. Sustained aerobic effort at altitude in that range is a poor idea. Above 200, Very Unhealthy, the official language is a health alert: the risk of health effects is increased for everyone.

Ranchers: how does wild fire tracking protect stock?

Ranchers use a wildfire tracker differently from homeowners because livestock cannot be evacuated in ten minutes and pasture cannot be evacuated at all. The operative question is not whether a fire will reach the house but which pasture, which water source and which access gate is in the path.

On the southern Plains that calculation is compressed by grass-fire speed. Texas A&M Forest Service, the lead wildfire agency for the state, recorded 1,078 wildfires for 301,528 acres in 2026 through early September, and NIFC forecast above-normal significant fire potential across Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi and southwestern Alabama for that month.

Track wind and burn bans, not just fires

The two most useful layers for an agricultural operation are the National Weather Service fire-weather alerts and, off-map, the county burn ban status. Each Texas county sets and lifts its own burn ban through the county judge or commissioners court, and neighbouring counties can be on opposite settings on the same day.

Equipment is the second-largest human cause of wildfire in Texas after debris burning, and haying, baling and welding season overlaps precisely with peak fine-fuel dryness. A wild fire tracker showing a Red Flag Warning across your county is a reason to postpone equipment work, not just a reason to watch the horizon.

Build a fire day protocolDecide in advance which pastures you move stock out of at which trigger, where the water and gate keys are, and who calls whom. Post the plan where every hand can see it. On a Red Flag day, move stock to grazed-down or worked ground before the wind picks up, not after a fire appears on the map.

What the map can and cannot tell you about pasture

A wildfire tracker shows fire location, official perimeter where one exists, and heat detections. Agricultural wild fire tracking has to fill the rest in from local knowledge. It does not show fuel load, fence status or road conditions. Satellite pixels are also coarse for agricultural scale: NOAA cautions that fire pixels do not translate into absolute fire area and should serve as a coarse indicator only.

That caution matters most on rangeland, where a single VIIRS pixel covers roughly 375 meters on a side and a GOES pixel considerably more. Use the wildfire tracker's hotspot cluster to know that something is burning and roughly where. Use the official perimeter, if one exists, to judge how much ground has actually been affected.

Journalists: how do you source a wildfire tracker?

Reporters need a wildfire tracker for speed and a paper trail for publication, which makes newsroom wildfire tracking a two-tool job. The workflow is therefore two-stage: use the map to find and rank incidents in seconds, then follow the official links in the detail panel to the agency record you will actually cite.

Never cite a map as the source of a number. Cite the agency. A wildfire tracker exists to get you to that agency record quickly, which is why each incident carries official links, a "Search on InciWeb" action, and explicit timestamps for the last NIFC update and the last satellite heat detection.

Timestamps are the reporting discipline that prevents the most common wildfire error. A story that says a fire is 40 percent contained without saying when that figure was published is describing a moment, not a state. NOAA notes that HMS products are timestamped in UTC, so subtract 5, 6, 7 or 8 hours for Eastern, Central, Mountain or Pacific time, and one hour fewer during daylight saving time.

How accurate is a wildfire tracker, and what lags?

A wildfire tracker is accurate about what it says and imprecise about what you infer, and honest wildfire tracking depends on knowing which is which. Each layer carries its own known error: satellite geolocation offsets, detection thresholds, processing latency and agency update schedules. NOAA publishes those figures openly, and understanding them turns a map from a rumour into an instrument.

Start with latency, because it is the number people guess wrong most often. NOAA reports that GOES full-disk Level 1 data becomes available within roughly 20 to 30 minutes of observation, while the contiguous US imaging sector is usually available within 10 to 15 minutes, with fire processing adding another 5 to 15 minutes.

Polar-orbiting instruments are slower. NOAA states that MODIS and VIIRS Level 1 data becomes available within about 1 hour 30 minutes to 2 hours 30 minutes of observation, with fire processing adding a further 5 to 10 minutes. Quality-controlled HMS output can add another 1 to 3 hours on average while image analysts assess the data.

What satellites can and cannot see

NOAA states there is no absolute size above which a fire will be detected. Traditional algorithms used with MODIS, VIIRS and GOES begin to respond to active fires occupying at least 0.01 percent of the pixel footprint, assuming an average fire temperature of at least 800 K. For a 1 km pixel that means an active area of at least 100 square meters.

Detection performance degrades at high view angles, because pixels enlarge toward the edge of the swath. It also degrades under cloud, under dense forest canopy and near water bodies where the land-water separation may be imperfect. A small, smouldering, canopy-covered fire under an overcast sky may simply not appear.

There is also a peculiar failure mode worth knowing. NOAA describes cases in which the VIIRS 375 m product detected part of a very tall, superheated smoke plume alongside the surface fire during explosive growth at night, with parallax displacing those pixels laterally away from the true perimeter. Analysts correct these when they can.

Fire Radiative Power, in contextNOAA explains that Fire Radiative Power relates directly to the rate of biomass consumption during a fire's active phase, and that the highest FRP values in a perimeter indicate the most intense segments. The agency also cautions against absolute comparisons: a pixel reading 50 MW could be the most intense part of a small grassland burn or the least intense part of a large wildfire. Details are on the NOAA Hazard Mapping System product page.

The false-positive list you should memorize

NOAA lists the main causes of commission errors in satellite fire products: fresh burn scars and sandy soils that elevate the mid-infrared signal, sun glint on optically bright or specular surfaces such as large solar panel clusters and metallic rooftops, and thermal anomalies from industrial activity including steel mills and gas flaring.

Structure fires in urban environments can also appear. Most of these are removed from the quality-controlled HMS product, but raw feeds carry them. This is the technical reason a wildfire tracker or forest fire tracker should apply a probability score rather than plotting every detection as a fire, and why weak signals below 25 percent are hidden by default in US Wildfire Tracker.

Why a fire can look active after it is out

Three mechanisms produce this illusion. First, an incident record stays published until the agency closes it, so an old record can persist. Second, residual heat in duff, stumps and heavy fuels keeps radiating long after flaming stops, which is routine in Alaskan black spruce and in the Minnesota Arrowhead, where NIFC reported deep-burning organics holding heat through repeated rainfall.

Third, detections accumulate. A cluster on the map may represent several overpasses across two days rather than simultaneous burning. The fix is to read the wildfire tracker's "last satellite heat" and "last GOES heat" timestamps rather than counting dots, and to switch outdated records off unless you are specifically researching history.

Wild fire tracking has a vocabulary borrowed from three professions, and a wildfire tracker surfaces terms from all of them: firefighting, meteorology and remote sensing. The area inside the fire's mapped perimeter, which includes unburned islands. Not the same as acres consumed.

Controlled
A later stage than contained: the line is complete and holding, and the fire is not expected to escape.

When the fire was first reported to an agency, which may be well after ignition.

Frequently Asked Questions About Wildfire Trackers

What is a wildfire tracker?

A wildfire tracker is a live map that merges official agency incident records, mapped fire perimeters, satellite thermal detections and National Weather Service fire-weather alerts into one view. US Wildfire Tracker adds a probability score to each detection so you can tell a confirmed incident from an unverified heat signal. As a wild fire tracker it is free, works in any browser and requires no account.

Is a wild fire tracker the same as a fire map?

Not quite. A fire map usually shows one layer, such as incident points or perimeters, with no scoring. A wild fire tracker fuses several sources and keeps them updating, then tells you how fresh each one is. The distinguishing feature is the timestamp set: last agency update, last satellite heat and last geostationary heat, shown separately rather than blended into a single claim.

How often does a wildfire tracker update?

Different layers of a wildfire tracker move at different speeds. US Wildfire Tracker refreshes automatically every few minutes while open. Underneath, NOAA reports GOES fire data arriving roughly 15 to 30 minutes after observation, MODIS and VIIRS taking about 1.5 to 2.5 hours plus processing, and analyst-reviewed HMS output adding 1 to 3 hours. Agency incident records update on operational schedules, often twice a day.

Which US region has the most wildfires each year?

The Southern Area, by a wide margin in fire count. NIFC recorded 36,040 human-caused wildfires there in 2025 plus 1,048 lightning-caused ones, more than any western geographic area. Western fires are typically far larger, so the West dominates acres burned while the Southeast dominates ignition counts. Both patterns matter when configuring a wildfire tracker for your region.

Can a wildfire tracker detect small fires?

Sometimes. NOAA states that satellite fire algorithms respond to fires covering at least 0.01 percent of a pixel at around 800 K or hotter, meaning about 100 square meters for a 1 km pixel. VIIRS at 375 meters per pixel catches many small fires MODIS misses. Cloud cover, dense canopy and high view angles can still hide a genuine small fire completely.

Why does a hotspot appear where there is no fire?

Because heat is not always wildfire. NOAA lists fresh burn scars, sandy soils, sun glint on solar panels and metal roofs, and industrial sources such as steel mills and gas flaring among known false alarms. Prescribed burns produce genuine heat too, especially in southern pine in winter. That is why detections below 25 percent probability are hidden by default in US Wildfire Tracker.

Does a wildfire tracker work in all 50 states?

Yes. US Wildfire Tracker offers a national view and a state filter covering all 50 states plus the District of Columbia, because the underlying NIFC, NASA and NOAA feeds are national. Wildfire tracking coverage quality varies with agency reporting: heavily federal regions such as the Southwest produce more mapped perimeters, while fast small fires in the Southeast may close before a perimeter is ever drawn.

When is wildfire season in each part of the US?

There is no single season. The Southeast peaks February to April and again in autumn, the Southwest April to early July, the Great Basin and Rockies June to September, the Pacific Northwest July to September, Alaska May to early August, and Southern California May through December. The Great Plains can burn in any dry, windy period, including winter.

What does percent contained actually mean?

It is the share of the fire's perimeter secured by completed control line, not the share extinguished. A fire at 100 percent containment may still burn actively inside its boundary for weeks. The later stages are controlled, meaning the line is holding and escape is not expected, and out, meaning no heat remains anywhere inside the perimeter.

Is wild fire tracking useful for smoke and air quality?

Very. Smoke travels hundreds of miles, and NIFC noted heavy smoke from Washington and Oregon suppressing surface heating in Montana and Idaho for days in August 2026. Pair a wildfire tracker's smoke overlay with the EPA Air Quality Index, where 101 to 150 affects sensitive groups, 151 to 200 can affect the general public, and 201 to 300 is an official health alert for everyone.

Does a fire tracker share my location?

No. Browser geolocation is used only to find fires near you and show distances in miles. US Wildfire Tracker does not store, sell or share your location. If you prefer not to grant permission at all, use the state filter or the national view instead, which requires no location access and still shows every reported incident.

Can I rely on a wildfire tracker for evacuation decisions?

No. A wildfire tracker is informational and is not an official emergency service. Evacuation orders and warnings come from your county sheriff, fire district or emergency management agency, and they always take precedence. Use a wildfire tracker to notice a threat early and to understand its direction, then act on the instructions of local authorities.

What is a forest fire tracker, and is it different?

A forest fire tracker is the same wildfire tracker described by fuel type rather than by data source. The distinction only matters in practice because timber fires and grass fires behave differently: timber fires last longer and update on slower perimeter cycles, while grass fires can outrun their own satellite detection latency. One wildfire tracker handles both, but wild fire tracking in timber and in grass should be read with different expectations.

How many wildfires does the US average per year?

Averaging the ten years 2016 through 2025 in NIFC's published table gives roughly 63,200 wildfires and about 7.05 million acres burned per year. Individual years vary widely: 2020 saw 10,122,336 acres from 58,950 fires, while 2023 saw only 2,693,910 acres from 56,580 fires. NIFC cautions that pre-1983 data was not collected under current reporting processes.
