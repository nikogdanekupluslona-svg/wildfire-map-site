How did we evaluate each way to track fires?

We evaluated each approach against eight measurable criteria drawn from how people actually use a fire tracking app: latency in minutes, source authority, alert reliability, geographic coverage, cost over 12 months, permissions demanded, offline behavior and the number of actions needed to answer one question.

Every criterion below is testable. You can run the same tests yourself in an afternoon, and we recommend you do before a fire season starts rather than during one.

The eight criteria we scored

  1. Time to first answer. How many seconds pass between deciding to check and seeing a fire's distance from your address? We counted clicks, taps and page loads from a cold start.
  2. Data latency. How old is the newest heat detection or perimeter the tool can show? NASA publishes satellite active-fire detections for the United States and Canada in real time and globally within 3 hours of observation, so anything slower is a tool choice, not a physics limit.
  3. Source authority. Does the tool link back to the National Interagency Fire Center incident record, or does it only show an unverified thermal pixel?
  4. Alert reliability. Does a notification arrive when the device is asleep, when the browser is closed, and when cellular data is congested?
  5. Coverage. Does it work in all 50 states plus the District of Columbia, or only in the states the vendor prioritized?
  6. Twelve-month cost. Subscription, in-app purchases, advertising load and the hidden cost of data resale.
  7. Permissions. What does the tool demand — precise location, contacts, background location, notification tokens — and what does it say it does with them?
  8. Degraded-mode behavior. What appears on screen when the feed fails, the network drops or the power goes out?

We deliberately excluded two things. We did not score visual polish, because a prettier interface does not change a perimeter. We also did not score social features, because crowd-sourced sightings are not a substitute for an official record and can spread errors faster than corrections.

Scale matters when you interpret those criteria. The National Interagency Fire Center recorded 77,850 wildland fires that burned 5,131,474 acres in 2025, and across the ten years from 2016 through 2025 its table averages roughly 63,200 fires and about 7.05 million acres per year. Any tool you choose has to stay legible when several hundred incidents are burning at once.

The exposure side of the equation has grown just as fast. US Forest Service researchers found that houses in the wildland-urban interface rose 46 percent between 1990 and 2020, from 30 million to 44.1 million, and that 32 percent of all US housing now sits inside that interface on 9.4 percent of the continental land area.

The interface is not only a western problemMiranda Mockrin, a research scientist with the US Forest Service Northern Research Station, notes that "the proportion of housing falling into WUI is high across much of the United States, especially within the Intermountain West, Northeast, and Southeast, where over 40% of housing is in the WUI." She adds that "in the most fireprone WUI areas, it is not a question of if but of when a wildfire will strike." Source: USDA Forest Service Research.

Is a side-panel extension the best fire tracking app?

A browser side-panel extension is the strongest general-purpose fire tracking app for anyone who spends working hours at a computer during fire season. The panel docks beside whatever you are reading, survives tab switching, and refreshes official and satellite feeds without you touching it.

Google's own extension documentation confirms the behavior that makes this format work: the Chrome side panel "remains open when navigating between tabs (if set to do so)," and the API has been available since Chrome 114. A fire map in that slot is glanceable in a way a buried tab never is.

Key advantages of a side-panel extension

The first advantage is persistence. Because the panel does not compete with your work for screen real estate, you check it fifteen times a day instead of twice, and you notice a new detection while it is still one pixel rather than a headline.

The second is fusion. US Wildfire Tracker merges four independent feeds into a single marker set, so one click tells you whether a heat signature has a matching NIFC incident record, how fresh the official perimeter is, and whether the National Weather Service has a Red Flag Warning over the same county.

The third is the fire probability score. Each detection receives a probability tier that combines whether an official NIFC record is linked, how recent that record is, and how recent the VIIRS, MODIS or GOES heat is. Weak signals below 25 percent probability are hidden by default and can be switched on when you want the raw view.

The fourth is desktop ergonomics. A 27-inch monitor shows a county-scale perimeter and a fire list side by side. A phone shows one or the other. When you are comparing three fires and two wind forecasts, that difference is not cosmetic.

Limitations of the extension approach

An extension only runs where a Chromium browser runs. It is not the tool you carry into a canyon with no laptop, and it cannot ring a phone that is face-down on a nightstand at 2 a.m. That job belongs to official phone alerts, covered later in this comparison.

An extension also depends on the browser being awake. If the machine is asleep or the browser is closed, no refresh happens, and the panel shows the last saved results when it wakes. The extension states this behavior openly rather than pretending the data is always live.

Finally, the extension is not an official emergency service. It surfaces evacuation orders, warnings and road closures from official alerts, but the order that matters legally is the one issued by your county sheriff or emergency management agency.

What makes the extension approach stand out

Two design decisions separate a serious extension from a novelty. The first is honest timestamps: US Wildfire Tracker shows "Last NIFC update," "Last satellite heat" and "Last GOES heat" separately, so you can see which part of the picture is stale. The second is the option to reveal weak signals and outdated records instead of silently discarding them.

The extension also carries the practical extras you need at the moment of decision: distance filtering with "fires near me," a fire list panel with search, a state filter covering all 50 states plus DC, a shareable link for a single fire, an InciWeb lookup, and directions away from a fire through Google Maps, Apple Maps or Waze.

When to choose the extension approach

Choose it when your default screen is a computer, when you need to watch more than one incident at a time, and when you want the official record and the satellite signal in the same click. When is a standalone mobile wildfire app the right pick?

A standalone mobile wildfire app earns its place when the decision you need to make is physical: pack the car, wake the household, move livestock, turn around on a highway. Phones travel, they have GPS, and they can push a notification through a locked screen.

Reach explains the popularity. Pew Research Center's 2025 survey found that 91 percent of US adults own a smartphone, and 16 percent of US adults are smartphone-only internet users, meaning they have no home broadband to fall back on.

Key advantages of a mobile wildfire app

Portability is the obvious one. A phone in a pocket is the only fire tracking app that comes with you to the hardware store, the trailhead and the evacuation center.

Push notification is the second. A well-built mobile app can wake a device for a proximity event, which no browser tool can do once the browser is closed. That single capability is why we do not tell readers to abandon phone tools.

Location fidelity is the third. Phone GPS is typically accurate to a few meters outdoors, far better than the coarse network-based position a desktop browser reports. For a "how far is it from me" question asked from a moving vehicle, that precision matters.

Limitations of the mobile app approach

Screen size is the first constraint. A perimeter that is legible at 27 inches becomes a smear at 6 inches, and comparing three incidents means three separate screens instead of one glance.

Battery and connectivity are the second. Background location polling is the most expensive thing an app can do to a battery, and phones lose signal exactly where fires burn: canyons, forest roads and areas where towers have lost commercial power.

Business model is the third. Free mobile tools are frequently funded by advertising, and advertising on mobile usually means a software development kit that reads device identifiers and approximate location. Read the data-safety disclosure before you install anything that asks for background location.

Fragmentation is the fourth. Many mobile tools cover a handful of states well and the rest thinly, so a tool that works on a California fire may show nothing useful for a fire in South Carolina or Oklahoma.

What makes the mobile approach stand out

The mobile format stands out for exactly one job: converting a change in the world into a sound in your pocket. Nothing else on this list does that as directly. Everything else the format offers — the map, the perimeter, the acreage — a browser does at least as well and usually better.

When to choose the mobile approach

Choose it as a complement, not a replacement, and choose it for the notification rather than the map. If you are trying to answer whether there is a fire near you right now, a phone gives you the fastest position fix; a larger screen then gives you the better interpretation.

Can a web fire map replace a fire tracking app?

A web fire map opened in a browser tab can replace an installed fire tracking app for occasional checks, and it is the most portable option on this list because it needs no installation, no store account and no particular operating system. What it cannot do is stay visible.

Key advantages of a web fire map

Zero friction is the headline. A link opens on a colleague's laptop, a library terminal or a phone in a vehicle without an install prompt, which makes it the only approach you can hand to another person mid-conversation.

Shareability is the second advantage. Because the view lives at a URL, you can send a specific fire to a neighbor, a group chat or a reporter, and they will see the same incident you are looking at rather than a screenshot with no timestamp.

Policy compatibility is the third. Managed corporate and school devices frequently block extension installs by policy while leaving normal web browsing open, so a web map is often the only fire tracking app a work machine will run.

Cross-platform parity is the fourth. The same page renders on Windows, macOS, Linux, ChromeOS, iOS and Android, which removes the coverage gaps that come from a vendor shipping one mobile platform first.

Limitations of the browser-tab approach

Tabs get buried. The practical failure mode is not that the map stops working; it is that you stop looking at it, which is precisely the failure a side panel prevents.

A closed tab also cannot notify you. Web notifications require the page to be open or a service worker to be registered and permitted, and most people never grant that permission, so a tab is a pull tool rather than a push tool.

Mobile browsers add a further constraint: aggressive tab discarding. Phones reclaim memory by unloading background tabs, so the map you left open at breakfast may reload from scratch at lunch and lose your filter settings.

What makes the web map approach stand out

The standout property is universality. There is no version to update, no store review queue between a fix and your screen, and no device you cannot reach. For a household that wants one link on the refrigerator whiteboard, that is a real advantage.

It is also the fastest way to check a fire on someone else's device during an evacuation, when your own phone may be at 4 percent battery. Our layer-by-layer walkthrough of a fire tracker map explains how to read perimeters, hotspots and probability tiers once the page is open.

When to choose the browser-tab approach

Choose it when you check fires occasionally, when device policy blocks extensions, or when you need to show someone else what you are seeing. Choose the extension instead when you need the same information visible all day without a deliberate act of remembering.

Are official agency pages better than a fire alert app?

Official agency incident pages are more authoritative than any consumer fire alert app and slower than all of them. They are where acreage, containment percentage, personnel counts, closures and incident narratives become official, usually once or twice a day rather than continuously.

This category includes the interagency incident information system commonly called InciWeb, National Interagency Fire Center situation reporting, National Weather Service fire-weather products, and state forestry and county emergency management notices. They are records of decisions, not live feeds of fire behavior.

Key advantages of official agency pages

Authority is the whole point. When the National Interagency Fire Center reported the national picture on September 9, 2026, it listed 59 large fires being managed, 14,405 firefighters and support personnel assigned, and 53,802 fires for more than 8.4 million acres year to date, at national preparedness level 3 on a scale of 1 to 5. No consumer product generates numbers like those; they only republish them.

Legal weight is the second advantage. An evacuation order, a forest closure order and a burn ban are legal instruments. The agency page carries the actual text, the boundaries and the effective dates, which a map icon summarizes at best.

Context is the third. Incident narratives explain what firefighters are doing, where the containment lines are being built and what the next operational period will look like — information no thermal pixel contains.

Limitations of the agency-page approach

Latency is the first limitation. A page updated at the morning briefing can be six hours behind a wind shift, and fires do not wait for operational periods.

Fragmentation is the second. A single fire may touch a federal incident page, a state forestry release, two county emergency accounts and an NWS fire-weather product, none of which are on the same page or the same schedule.

Discoverability is the third. Finding the right page for a fire whose name you do not yet know is genuinely hard during the first hours, which is the exact window when speed matters most.

What makes agency pages stand out

Agency data is also machine-readable, which is why good consumer tools exist at all. The National Weather Service publishes an open API whose documentation states that "all of the information presented via the API is intended to be open data, free to use for any purpose," with an alerts endpoint that "contains alerts issued over the past seven days" and a required User-Agent header.

That openness is the reason a fused fire tracking app can show a Red Flag Warning polygon next to a satellite detection: the underlying products are public. Our explainer on how wildfire tracking works end to end follows an incident from first detection through the interagency record system.

When to choose the agency-page approach

Choose it for verification, never for first detection. The workflow that works is to see the change in a fire tracking app, then open the official record to confirm acreage, containment and closures before you repeat the number to anyone else.

Do raw satellite viewers beat a fire tracking app?

Raw satellite hotspot viewers show thermal anomalies before anyone has confirmed them, which makes them the earliest signal available to the public and the easiest one to misread. They beat a consumer fire tracking app on speed and lose to it on interpretation.

The category covers agency-run viewers that plot detections from NASA's MODIS and VIIRS sensors and NOAA's geostationary imagers, plus the same detections delivered as data files, map services or email subscriptions to anyone who registers.

Key advantages of raw satellite viewers

Speed is the first advantage, and it is substantial. NASA states that its active-fire detections are available globally within 3 hours of satellite observation, and in real time for the United States and Canada, which is faster than most agency narratives are written.

Resolution is the second. Each VIIRS active-fire pixel represents a 375-meter square, roughly 1,230 feet on a side, while MODIS bands 8 through 36 resolve at 1,000 meters from an orbit altitude of 705 kilometers. Finer pixels mean smaller fires are detectable and perimeters are better defined.

Cadence is the third. Geostationary imagers refresh far faster than polar orbiters: the GOES Advanced Baseline Imager in its default scan mode produces "a full disk image every 10 minutes, a CONUS/PACUS image every five minutes, and images from both mesoscale domains every 60 seconds," with mesoscale coverage over a 1,000-by-1,000-kilometer box at 30-second intervals in continuous mode. Sixteen spectral bands resolve at 0.5 to 2 kilometers depending on wavelength.

Subscription flexibility is the fourth. NASA's fire alert service lets you draw a custom region or polygon and receive detections in near real time or as daily or weekly summaries, from MODIS, VIIRS on Suomi NPP, NOAA-20 and NOAA-21.

Limitations of raw satellite viewers

False positives are the first and largest limitation. A thermal anomaly is a hot pixel, not a wildfire. Flares, steel mills, solar arrays, agricultural burning and permitted prescribed fires all register, and NOAA notes that its analysts intervene when detections appear outside actual fire areas.

Orbital gaps are the second. Terra crosses the equator at about 10:30 a.m. on a descending node and Aqua at about 1:30 p.m. on an ascending node, so a fire that starts at 3 p.m. may wait hours for a polar pass even though a geostationary imager sees the region continuously.

Cloud, canopy and smoke are the third. Thermal sensors cannot see through thick cloud, and small or smoldering fires under dense canopy can be missed entirely, which is why an empty map is never proof that nothing is burning.

Interpretation burden is the fourth. Raw viewers hand you confidence values and fire radiative power without telling you whether an incident record exists, whether anyone has been evacuated, or whether the heat is a week old.

What makes satellite viewers stand out

They stand out because they are the upstream truth that everything else depends on. Every consumer fire tracking app, including US Wildfire Tracker, is downstream of these detections; the difference is whether a tool hands you the pixel or interprets it.

That interpretation is exactly what the fire probability score does. By fusing the presence and freshness of an NIFC record with the recency of VIIRS, MODIS and GOES heat, the extension converts a raw detection into a tier you can act on, hides weak signals below 25 percent by default, and warns explicitly that hotspots can reflect prescribed burns or industrial heat.

When to choose raw satellite viewers

Choose them when you are doing analysis rather than making a household decision, when you need a custom polygon subscription for a specific parcel, or when you want to verify that a consumer tool is not filtering something you care about. Can phone and SMS alerts replace a fire alert app?

Phone-based alerting is the only approach on this list that reaches you without an app, an account or an internet connection, and it is the only one that will wake a sleeping household. It cannot show you a fire's shape, size or direction, so it complements a fire alert app rather than replacing one.

The category has three tiers: federally standardized Wireless Emergency Alerts broadcast from cell towers, county opt-in systems that send SMS, voice and email to registered addresses, and public safety radio audio streamed or received on a scanner.

Key advantages of phone and radio alerting

No installation is the first advantage. The National Weather Service is explicit that Wireless Emergency Alerts require no signup and arrive "without need to download an app or subscribe to a service," and that the service is offered free by wireless carriers.

Congestion resistance is the second. Because alerts are broadcast from towers rather than sent as individual messages, "WEA messages are not affected by network congestion" even when calls and texts are failing.

Precision has improved sharply. FEMA describes enhanced geo-targeting that reaches "100 percent of the targeted area with no more than 1/10th of a mile (528 feet) overshoot," and message length rose from 90 characters to 360 characters on LTE and newer networks. Spanish-language alerts were added in December 2019, and newer devices retain alerts for at least 24 hours.

Independence from the internet is the fourth advantage, and it is the reason to keep a scanner or a battery radio: broadcast reception does not depend on a working data connection or a functioning home router.

Limitations of alert-only approaches

No ambient picture is the first limitation. An alert tells you an action is required in a polygon; it does not tell you the fire is nine miles upwind and running, which is the information that lets you leave before the order arrives.

Coverage gaps are the second. Wireless alerts require cell coverage at your location, and county opt-in systems require you to have registered the right address and phone number in advance — something most people discover they never did.

Scanner audio adds its own problems: many agencies now encrypt tactical channels, transmissions use jargon and unit numbers rather than street names, and a single misheard sentence can send a household in the wrong direction.

What makes alerting stand out

Alerting stands out on reliability rather than richness. It is the layer with the fewest dependencies, the widest reach and the shortest latency, and it is the only one on this list that is legally authoritative at the moment it arrives.

When to choose alert systems

Always. Alerts are not an alternative to a fire tracking app; they are the floor beneath one. Leave Wireless Emergency Alerts enabled on every phone in the household, register every address with your county system, and keep a battery-powered radio with the go-kit.

Key differences between fire tracking app approaches

Five trade-offs separate these six approaches, and every one of them is a genuine trade rather than a ranking. Understanding which side of each trade you need is more useful than memorizing feature lists, because your needs change between June and an active evacuation.

Speed versus certainty

Satellite viewers are fastest and least certain; agency pages are slowest and most certain. A fused fire tracking app sits deliberately in the middle by showing the fast signal and labeling how well it is corroborated. If you only remember one trade-off, remember this one.

Push versus pull

Alert systems and mobile apps push; extensions, web maps, agency pages and satellite viewers pull. Push wakes you but tells you little; pull tells you everything but only when you remember to look. A side panel narrows the gap because looking costs one glance rather than one decision.

Breadth versus depth

A national tool that covers all 50 states and DC will rarely carry the neighborhood-level closure detail a county emergency page publishes. A county page will not tell you that a second fire started 40 miles upwind. Running one of each is not redundancy; it is coverage.

Free versus funded

Every approach here has a free tier, but they are funded differently: taxes fund agency data, subscriptions fund some mobile tools, advertising funds others, and US Wildfire Tracker is free with no ads and no third-party ad trackers inside the extension. The funding model predicts the permission requests better than the price does.

Interpretation versus raw data

The last trade-off is how much thinking the tool does for you. A raw viewer hands you a confidence percentage; a probability score hands you a tier; an evacuation order hands you an instruction. Match the level of interpretation to how much time you have to think.

Which alert types matter most in a wildfire app?

Four alert families matter in a wildfire app, and they arrive on different clocks: fire-weather alerts hours to days ahead, new-detection alerts within minutes to hours, official incident updates once or twice a day, and evacuation instructions in seconds. Confusing them is the most common user error we see.

The distinction is not academic. A Red Flag Warning is a forecast about conditions, not a report of fire. A satellite detection is a report of heat, not of danger. An evacuation order is an instruction with legal force. Each one changes what you should do next.

Fire-weather alerts are the earliest useful warning

The National Weather Service issues a Red Flag Warning "in conjunction with land management agencies, to alert land managers to an ongoing or imminent critical fire weather pattern," while a Fire Weather Watch "alerts land managers and the public that upcoming weather conditions could result in extensive wildland fire occurrence or extreme fire behavior."

The thresholds are specific. The NWS glossary defines Red Flag criteria as a fire danger rating of high to extreme plus a forecast of sustained winds averaging 15 mph or greater, relative humidity at or below 25 percent, and temperature above 75 degrees Fahrenheit, with dry lightning and unstable air added in some states. Warnings are issued when the forecaster has high confidence the criteria will occur within 24 hours.

A fire tracking app that draws those polygons on the same map as the fires converts an abstract forecast into a spatial question: is your county inside the polygon, and are there detections upwind of you inside it?

Proximity alerts and how far is far enough

Proximity alerting is the feature people most often mean when they ask for a fire alert app. US Wildfire Tracker offers proximity notifications for new incidents near you when location is enabled, and surfaces evacuation orders, warnings and road closures from official alerts along with smoke and air-quality overlays.

Choosing a radius is a judgment about terrain and wind, not a universal number. In flat terrain with light wind, a 10-mile ring gives most households hours. On a wind-driven run through aligned canyons, 25 miles can compress to a single afternoon, which is why we suggest widening the radius whenever a Red Flag Warning covers your area.

A practical radius ladderSet 5 miles for "act now," 10 miles for "prepare," 25 miles for "pay attention," and 50 miles for smoke and travel planning. Widen every ring by one step during a Red Flag Warning, and treat any detection upwind of you as one ring closer than it is.

Smoke and air-quality alerts are a separate clock

Smoke arrives on its own schedule and can affect people hundreds of miles from any fire. EPA's Air Quality Index uses six color-coded categories, where an AQI of 100 "generally corresponds to an ambient air concentration that equals the level of the short-term national ambient air quality standard for protection of public health."

The pollutant that matters in smoke is fine particulate matter. EPA defines PM2.5 as "fine inhalable particles, with diameters that are generally 2.5 micrometers and smaller," and notes that "the average human hair is about 70 micrometers in diameter — making it 30 times larger than the largest fine particle." Particles that small travel deep into the lungs, which is why a smoke alert can matter in a state with no active fires.

Why timestamps beat refresh buttons

A refresh button tells you when the app last asked. A timestamp tells you when the world last answered. A well-built extension shows "Last NIFC update," "Last satellite heat" and "Last GOES heat" as separate values precisely because those three clocks routinely disagree by hours.

Read them in that order. If the NIFC update is 14 hours old but GOES heat is 6 minutes old, the fire is active and the paperwork is behind. If satellite heat is two days old and the NIFC record says 95 percent contained, the marker is a historical artifact rather than a live threat.

Polar orbits explain most of the gaps

Polar-orbiting satellites fly at roughly 435 miles (700 kilometers) and provide up to twice-daily global coverage, while geostationary satellites deliver five to 15-minute repeat imaging. MODIS flies at 705 kilometers with a 2,330-kilometer cross-track swath, which is why a single pass covers a very wide strip but only for a moment.

That geometry produces the pattern users notice as "the map went quiet." Nothing broke; the satellite simply is not overhead. This is also why a tool that carries GOES heat alongside polar detections has fewer blind minutes than one that carries only VIIRS.

An empty map is not an all-clearThermal sensors miss small or smoldering fires under cloud or dense canopy, and detections can lag by minutes to hours. Treat a blank area as "no confirmed detection in the last pass," not as "nothing is burning." Local smoke, sirens and a phone alert all outrank a quiet map.

What works when cell signal and power go out?

Cellular coverage, home broadband and grid power all fail in the same conditions that make fires dangerous, so a fire tracking app plan that assumes connectivity is a plan that fails at the worst moment. The design question is what still functions with no data connection and no wall power.

Wildfires damage the same infrastructure everyone depends on: towers lose commercial power and run on backup batteries or generators, fiber runs burn, and utilities de-energize lines pre-emptively during extreme fire weather. Any of those alone can take your fire tracking app offline.

Degrade gracefully rather than fail blank

The behavior that matters here is what the screen shows when the feed does not answer. The extension keeps the last successful results on screen when a refresh fails rather than clearing the map, so a stale answer with a visible timestamp replaces no answer at all.

Build a three-layer redundancy stack

  1. Layer one, always on. Wireless Emergency Alerts enabled on every phone, plus registration with your county's opt-in notification system for every address you care about.
  2. Layer two, ambient. A fire tracking app that stays visible on your main screen, with the state filter and distance ring already configured.
  3. Layer three, offline. A battery-powered radio, a printed map of two exit routes, a charged power bank, and a written list of the phone numbers for your county emergency office and non-emergency dispatch.

The redundancy principle here is independence, not duplication. Two apps that both need the same cellular tower are one layer, not two. A radio and an app are genuinely two.

Power planning is device planning

Laptops typically outlast phones on battery when both are showing the same map, because a phone doing background location and cellular searching in a weak-signal area burns power quickly. If a public safety power shutoff is announced, charge everything, then move the map to the device with the most runtime.

Ready.gov's guidance to "have enough supplies for your household, including a first aid kit, in your go bag or car trunk" applies to charging hardware too. A 20,000 mAh power bank is a cheap insurance policy against a two-day outage.

Broadcast alerting is the privacy-cheapest layer

Wireless Emergency Alerts are worth understanding on this dimension because they invert the usual model. FEMA notes that wireless providers "primarily use cell broadcast technology for WEA message delivery," and that alerts "are broadcast from area cell towers to mobile phones within the defined geographic location." The system finds the area, not you.

That is why alerts require no signup and no subscription. Nothing about your identity, your address or your movement is transmitted to receive one, which makes broadcast alerting the only approach here with essentially zero privacy cost.

Three questions to ask before granting location

  1. Foreground or background? Background location is a continuous movement log. Grant it only when a tool genuinely needs to notify you while closed, and revoke it after the season if you stop using the tool.
  2. Stored or transient? Ask whether the position is used to compute a distance and discarded, or written to a profile. A tool that requires an account is more likely to do the latter.
  3. Who else sees it? Advertising software development kits inside free apps often receive location as a side effect. No ads means one fewer party in the chain.

None of this means you should refuse location entirely. Distance to a fire is the most useful single number a fire tracking app can give you, and computing it requires knowing roughly where you are. The goal is proportionality: precise enough to be useful, transient enough to be safe.

How do you set up each fire tracker app correctly?

Setup is where most fire tracker app value is won or lost, and almost all of it happens once. The checklists below take about twenty minutes in total and should be done before a fire season rather than during one.

Side-panel extension checklist

  1. Install US Wildfire Tracker from the Chrome Web Store on any Chromium browser that supports Chrome extensions.
  2. Pin the extension icon to the toolbar so opening the panel is one click rather than three.
  3. Open the side panel and confirm it stays docked while you switch tabs; the Chrome side panel supports this behavior from Chrome 114 onward.
  4. Allow location once so "fires near me" can compute distances in miles, then set your preferred distance ring.
  5. Set the state filter to your own state for daily use, and learn where the national view toggle is for travel days.
  6. Open one active incident and read all three timestamps — last NIFC update, last satellite heat, last GOES heat — so the layout is familiar before you need it.
  7. Toggle weak signals on once to see what is normally hidden below the 25 percent probability threshold, then decide whether you want them on by default.
  8. Practice the share link and the directions-away-from-a-fire option, so both are muscle memory.

Mobile and alert checklist

  1. Confirm Wireless Emergency Alerts are enabled in every phone's notification settings, including for older family members' devices.
  2. Register every address you care about with the county emergency notification system, including a work address and a school address.
  3. Decide deliberately whether any app gets background location, and revoke it from anything that does not need it.
  4. Turn off notification batching for emergency categories so an alert is not delayed by a focus mode.
  5. Add your county emergency management and non-emergency dispatch numbers to contacts, and write them on paper for the go-kit.

Verification checklist for any incident

  1. Read the probability tier before the acreage; a high-probability detection with a linked NIFC record is a different object from an isolated hot pixel.
  2. Compare the satellite heat timestamp with the NIFC update timestamp to see which half of the picture is stale.
  3. Check whether a Red Flag Warning polygon covers the fire or covers you, because wind is what turns a distant fire into a local one.
  4. Open the official incident record before repeating acreage or containment to anyone else.
  5. Look at wind direction, not just distance; a fire 20 miles upwind deserves more attention than one 8 miles downwind.

Where does every fire tracking app still fall short?

Every fire tracking app inherits the limits of its inputs, and no interface can fix a sensor that did not see something. Four limitations apply to all six approaches, and stating them plainly is more useful than any feature comparison.

Detection is probabilistic, not certain

Thermal sensors infer fire from radiance, so both false positives and false negatives are structural. Industrial heat sources, gas flares, agricultural burning and permitted prescribed fires generate detections that look identical to a wildfire start, and NOAA's Hazard Mapping System exists partly because analysts must correct automated output.

The practical consequence is that a lone detection with no official record and no smoke report is a question, not an answer. A responsible fire tracking app states this directly by warning that hotspots can reflect prescribed burns or industrial heat.

Perimeters are snapshots on an agency clock

An official perimeter is drawn from infrared flights, GPS ground mapping or satellite analysis at a specific time, then published on the agency's schedule. During a wind-driven run, the true edge can be miles beyond the last published line, which is why perimeters should be read as "at least this far" rather than "exactly this far."

Growth is not a straight line

Fire behavior responds to wind, slope, fuel moisture and time of day, and none of those are linear. NASA research has documented that warmer nights change the arithmetic: Doug Morton observed that "warmer nighttime temperature allow fires to burn through the night and burn more intensely, and that allows fires to spread over multiple days where previously, cooler nighttime temperatures might have weakened or extinguished the fire after only one day," in an analysis published by NASA Goddard.

Human ignition patterns are not on any map

Most US wildfires start with people, and no app predicts that. NIFC's cause statistics recorded 69,556 human-caused wildland fires in 2025 out of 77,850 total, which is roughly 89 percent by our calculation of the figures in NIFC's tables.

Jim Randerson, an Earth system scientist at the University of California, Irvine, described the mechanism in the same NASA analysis: "In the Western U.S., people are accidentally igniting fires all the time. But when we have a period of extreme weather, high temperatures, low humidity, then it's more likely that typical outdoor activity might lead to an accidental fire that quickly gets out of control and becomes a large wildfire."

That is the strongest argument for pairing a fire tracking app with fire-weather awareness. The app tells you what has ignited; the Red Flag Warning tells you what an ignition would do today.

What is the best fire tracking app for most people in 2026?

Is a fire tracking app free, or should you expect to pay?

How quickly does a fire tracking app show a brand-new fire?

It depends on which sensor sees it first. NASA publishes active-fire detections for the United States and Canada in real time and globally within about 3 hours of observation, while NOAA's geostationary imagers refresh a continental scan every five minutes and mesoscale sectors every 30 to 60 seconds. Official incident records usually lag those detections by hours because they follow agency reporting schedules.

Do you need a mobile app alongside a browser extension?

You need a notification path, which is not the same thing as a second map. Wireless Emergency Alerts require no download and no subscription, are free from carriers, and are unaffected by network congestion, so they cover the wake-me-up job. Add a mobile map only if you regularly need fire information away from a computer, such as during field work or long drives.

Why does a fire tracking app show heat where there is no fire?

Thermal sensors detect radiance, not flames. Gas flares, steel mills, agricultural burning and permitted prescribed fires all produce detections that resemble a wildfire start. Good tools handle this with a probability tier and an explicit warning; US Wildfire Tracker hides weak signals below 25 percent probability by default and states that hotspots can reflect prescribed burns or industrial heat.

Can an app to track fires work without an internet connection?

Not for new data. Maps need a connection to fetch detections and perimeters, though US Wildfire Tracker shows the last saved results rather than a blank screen when a refresh fails. Only broadcast alerting and radio work without data: Wireless Emergency Alerts are broadcast from cell towers, and a battery-powered radio needs no network at all.

Which fire tracker app is most accurate for perimeters?

Accuracy comes from the source, not the interface. Official interagency perimeters published through NIFC and WFIGS are the authoritative geometry, and any tool showing them is as accurate as its last sync. The differentiator is honesty about age: a tool that timestamps the perimeter separately from satellite heat lets you see when the official line is hours behind the fire.

What does the fire probability score actually measure?

It measures corroboration and freshness rather than danger. The score fuses whether an official NIFC record is linked to the detection, how recent that record and its perimeter are, and how recent the VIIRS, MODIS or GOES heat signal is. A high tier means several independent sources agree recently; a low tier means a single, possibly old or ambiguous signal.

How far from a fire should you start paying attention?

Distance alone is a weak signal; wind direction and terrain matter more. A workable ladder is 5 miles for immediate action, 10 miles for preparation, 25 miles for attention, and 50 miles for smoke and travel planning. Widen every ring during a Red Flag Warning, which the National Weather Service issues for sustained winds of 15 mph or more with humidity at or below 25 percent.

Does a wildfire app replace official evacuation orders?

No. Evacuation orders are legal instructions issued by county sheriffs and emergency management agencies, and Ready.gov's guidance is to evacuate immediately when authorities tell you to. A fire tracking app is for the hours before an order, when you decide whether to pre-pack, and the hours after, when you track whether a fire is moving toward the route you plan to use.

What privacy does a fire alert app require from you?

At minimum, an approximate position to compute distance. The important distinction is whether the position is used once and discarded or retained as a movement history. US Wildfire Tracker uses location only to find nearby fires and states that it is never stored, sold or shared, and the extension carries no ads and no third-party ad trackers.

Do fire tracking apps cover all 50 states equally?

The federal feeds do, but products vary. Satellite detections and NIFC records cover the entire country, so a tool built on them can offer all 50 states plus the District of Columbia. Vendor-specific tools sometimes prioritize a few western states, which is why a Southeast or Northeast reader should check coverage before a season rather than during a fire.

How often should you check a fire tracking app in fire season?

On a normal day, once in the morning is enough if alerts are enabled. During a Fire Weather Watch or Red Flag Warning, check every few hours, and during an active incident within your attention ring, check hourly. A docked side panel makes this cost one glance, which is why the format outperforms a buried tab in practice.

What to do in the first ten minutes of a nearby detection?

Read the probability tier, check whether an official NIFC record is linked, compare the satellite and agency timestamps, and note the wind direction relative to your address. Then confirm whether your county has issued anything, charge devices, and move vehicles so they face outward. Do not wait for a headline to confirm what the map already shows.

Are scanner audio and social posts worth adding to the mix?

Scanner audio is fast but hard to interpret, uses unit numbers rather than street names, and is increasingly encrypted on tactical channels. Social posts spread errors faster than corrections and rarely carry timestamps. Both are supplements at best; neither should influence an evacuation decision that an official order or a corroborated map view has not already supported.
